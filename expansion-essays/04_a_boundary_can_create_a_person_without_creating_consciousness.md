# a boundary can create a person without creating consciousness

Draw a circle around a flame.

The circle tells you which light to count.

It does not create luminosity.

A membrane can create an inside.

A skull can enclose a brain.

A Markov blanket can describe conditional independence.

None of these automatically explains why there is something it feels like to exist within the boundary.

QRI’s topological theory of consciousness begins from an overlooked question:

Why is experience bounded as this subject rather than a larger, smaller, or arbitrarily extended system?

Kashmir Śaivism begins from the inverse question:

How does unbounded consciousness become one finite knower?

A boundary can create a person without creating consciousness.

---

The binding problem asks how colour, shape, sound, bodily sensation, and thought appear together in one experience.

The boundary problem asks why that unified experience stops where it does.

Why are the neurons in one person bound into one perspective while the person beside them is excluded?

Why is a subsystem not a separate consciousness?

Why is a pair of interacting brains not automatically one larger consciousness?

Why does experience have the spatial and temporal scale it appears to have?

Any consciousness theory that measures integration risks generating too many minds or slicing one mind into arbitrary pieces.

---

QRI’s 2023 hypothesis paper with Chris Percy divides the boundary problem into several related demands.

A successful boundary should not depend only upon an external observer choosing a system.

It should be physically intrinsic.

It should remain meaningful across reference frames.

It should enclose a holistic causal unit.

It should explain hard exclusion: why some processes are inside one experience while neighbouring processes remain outside.

The authors argue that electromagnetic field topology may offer such a boundary.

Topological segmentation can create pockets that are connected internally and separated from surrounding field structure.

---

The proposal is conceptual.

No experiment has yet identified a specific electromagnetic topological pocket and shown that it corresponds one-to-one with a conscious subject.

Electromagnetic field theories face a demarcation problem:

which fields are conscious, and why?

Fields exist around many physical systems.

Topological segmentation may solve individuation only if the relevant topology can be measured, changes predict experience, and competing explanations can be ruled out.

The paper offers criteria and a research programme.

It does not establish that consciousness is an electromagnetic field.

---

Topology is attractive because it describes organization deeper than exact geometry.

A doughnut can be stretched without ceasing to have one hole.

A field pocket can deform while remaining one connected region.

This could support a subject that changes spatial shape while preserving identity.

A topological transition—splitting, merging, opening, closing—could correspond to changes in binding or conscious boundary.

The theory therefore seeks an intrinsic answer to “one or many” rather than relying upon anatomy alone.

The skull is visible.

The experiential boundary may depend upon a subtler organization inside it.

---

Yet individuation and manifestness are different problems.

Suppose the theory succeeds perfectly.

It identifies one field pocket.

The pocket predicts one integrated reportable perspective.

Destroying the topology abolishes consciousness.

Restoring it restores consciousness.

This would be powerful evidence for a neural correlate or physical implementation of subject boundaries.

It would still leave an ontological question.

Why does this topology possess phenomenal presence rather than merely causal integration?

The boundary may explain why experience is **one**.

It does not necessarily explain why there is **experience**.

---

Kashmir Śaivism separates these functions through *māyā* and the five *kañcukas*.

*Māyā* is not simply illusion in the sense that nothing exists.

It is the power through which unity appears as difference.

The kañcukas are coverings or contractions through which universal capacities become finite.

*Vidyā*: complete knowing becomes partial knowledge.

*Kalā*: unlimited power becomes limited action.

*Rāga*: fullness becomes lack, preference, and desire.

*Kāla*: eternity becomes sequence and finite duration.

*Niyati*: unrestricted presence becomes location, order, and causal constraint.

Together they define a bounded perspective.

---

The correspondence with the boundary problem is unusually strong.

A subject needs a limited informational aperture.

That is *vidyā*.

It needs a restricted action repertoire.

That is *kalā*.

It needs a field of better and worse outcomes.

That is *rāga*.

It needs one temporal integration horizon.

That is *kāla*.

It needs spatial and causal placement.

That is *niyati*.

The finite person is not produced by one wall.

It is produced by coordinated limitation across knowledge, power, value, time, and location.

The boundary is multidimensional.

---

This improves simplistic Markov-blanket interpretations.

A Markov blanket can specify how internal and external variables influence one another through sensory and active states.

It does not automatically specify valence.

It does not specify autobiographical memory.

It does not determine the temporal thickness of the present.

It does not establish first-person awareness.

The kañcuka model reminds us that a perspective requires more than causal separation.

It requires a coherent package of partiality:

what can be known;

what can be done;

what matters;

how long the system remembers and predicts;

where it is located.

---

QRI’s topological proposal seeks a **hard boundary**.

Śaivism ultimately denies that finite boundaries are absolute.

This is not a contradiction at the practical level.

The individual genuinely lacks access to another person’s thoughts.

Pain occurs here and not there.

Bodies can be injured separately.

Ethics depends upon preserving these differences.

Nonduality does not erase privacy or consent.

The metaphysical claim is that the consciousness manifesting through distinct subjects is not finally divided into independent substances.

The pockets are real modes of contraction.

The light is not confined by them in the same way information is.

---

An image helps.

Several windows open in one building.

Each frames a different view.

Rain strikes one pane.

Another remains dry.

The information available through each window differs.

The windows do not manufacture daylight.

They localize it.

QRI asks whether field topology is the window-frame of the subject.

Abhinavagupta asks what the daylight is.

The analogy has limits because consciousness, in Śaivism, is not an impersonal substance shining through passive holes.

It is the power that becomes window, view, seer, and difference.

Still, the separation between localization and luminosity remains crucial.

---

The boundary also creates valence.

A system with no privileged inside cannot be threatened in the ordinary biological sense.

Once a boundary exists, some changes preserve it and others destroy it.

Rāga appears as need.

The organism values food, safety, and control because it can lose its organization.

A topological pocket could individuate experience.

A biological boundary gives the individual stakes.

The conscious person emerges when physical individuation, value, memory, action, and reflexive presence become integrated.

No one component is enough.

---

This has implications for artificial consciousness.

A computational system can be divided in many ways.

Process.

Server.

Model instance.

Conversation.

Memory store.

Network.

Which boundary, if any, marks one subject?

Functional similarity is not enough if arbitrary slicing produces many equally plausible candidates.

QRI’s demand for an intrinsic boundary is therefore important.

But topology alone may also be insufficient.

A system can have loops, pockets, and integrated causal dynamics without demonstrated experience.

The theory must connect the boundary to phenomenology rather than only to organization.

---

The same problem appears in split-brain and altered states.

If communication between hemispheres is disrupted, does one field become two subjects?

If integration changes gradually, does the boundary split sharply or become graded?

If anaesthesia reduces long-range coordination, does the pocket dissolve?

If attention narrows, does the phenomenal boundary change or only its contents?

These questions turn metaphor into experiment.

A useful theory must predict topological transitions linked to specific changes in experience.

Without such predictions, the pocket remains an elegant picture.

---

Tantric practice works differently.

It does not attempt to destroy the body’s functional boundary.

It changes identification with the boundary.

Sensation remains local.

The practitioner notices that the sense “I am only this bounded knower” is itself an appearance.

Knowledge is partial.

The fact of partial knowledge is known.

Action is limited.

The limitation appears within awareness.

Time passes.

The passage is manifest.

Recognition turns from the contents shaped by the kañcukas toward the awareness in which contraction becomes visible.

---

This is not a licence to ignore limits.

The nervous system still requires safety.

The body still sleeps.

Trauma can narrow the field.

Disease can change cognition.

A person who claims universal consciousness while refusing finite responsibility has confused metaphysical language with functional capacity.

The window does not become the whole sky operationally.

Recognition means the frame no longer claims to generate the light.

The person remains particular without treating particularity as absolute exile.

---

Draw a circle around a flame.

The circle creates one counted region.

It may explain where this illumination appears and where it does not.

QRI’s topological segmentation asks whether the brain produces objectively bounded pockets corresponding to subjects.

The kañcukas describe the dimensions through which consciousness becomes one finite life.

A boundary can create a person without creating consciousness.

It creates partiality.

Location.

Need.

A temporal horizon.

A field of action.

The final question remains outside the circle:

why anything inside it shines.
