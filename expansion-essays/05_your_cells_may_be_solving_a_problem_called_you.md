# your cells may be solving a problem called you

Cut a planarian flatworm into pieces.

The fragments do not merely close their wounds.

They rebuild the correct missing structures, restore proportion, and stop when a complete body has returned.

The cells at the wound did not personally see the original animal.

They do not possess a tiny anatomical blueprint hidden in each nucleus.

Yet the collective behaves as though it knows what shape is missing.

Somewhere between the genome and the finished body, a problem is being solved.

---

Developmental biology has often been narrated as genetic execution.

DNA contains instructions.

Cells read them.

A body is assembled.

This is true at one level and misleading at another.

Genes specify proteins and regulatory interactions. They are indispensable. But the same genome can participate in different large-scale anatomical outcomes. Cells must interpret local signals, communicate across distances, correct errors, and coordinate growth toward a form much larger than any single cell.

A list of parts does not explain how the parts know when the whole is complete.

Michael Levin’s research program focuses on this gap.

What stores target morphology?

How do cell collectives detect deviation from it?

How do they act until the error is reduced?

---

One important medium is bioelectricity.

Every cell maintains a voltage across its membrane through ion channels and pumps. Neurons use rapid electrical activity for familiar forms of signaling, but non-neural cells also possess resting potentials and exchange electrical information through gap junctions.

Across tissues, these voltages form patterns.

The patterns interact with gene expression, neurotransmitter movement, cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation.

Bioelectric states are not ghostly fields floating beyond chemistry.

They are physiological properties generated by ordinary cellular machinery.

But their large-scale organization may carry information about anatomy.

Electricity becomes a language through which cells negotiate the body they belong to.

---

Planarian experiments make the idea vivid.

Briefly altering electrical communication during regeneration can cause some fragments to produce two-headed worms instead of the normal head-tail body plan. In certain experiments, the altered anatomy persists through later rounds of cutting and regeneration even after the original treatment is gone.

The genome remains the ordinary wild-type genome.

The regenerative outcome has changed.

Researchers describe this as a rewriteable pattern memory implemented partly through bioelectric networks.

The tissue remembers a different answer to the question:

How many heads should this body have?

---

“Memory” here is not necessarily conscious recollection.

A thermostat remembers a target temperature only in a minimal functional sense. A cell network can maintain a stable state, compare current conditions with that state, and act to restore it.

The provocative move is to place such processes on a continuum with cognition.

Memory.

Preference.

Error correction.

Goal pursuit.

These words are usually reserved for brains and organisms.

Levin asks whether their basic logic appears earlier, distributed across cellular collectives.

If cells cooperate toward a target morphology, perhaps cognition did not suddenly appear when neurons evolved.

Perhaps nervous systems amplified an older capacity of living matter to navigate problem spaces.

---

This framework is called basal cognition or diverse intelligence.

The claim is not that every cell thinks like a tiny human.

It is that “intelligence” can be defined through competencies rather than resemblance to us.

Can a system pursue goals across changing conditions?

Solve problems?

Integrate information?

Adapt when perturbed?

Generalize beyond one fixed response?

A bacterium navigating a chemical gradient has a tiny goal horizon.

A tissue maintaining organ shape has a larger one.

An animal planning across years has a larger one still.

The boundaries of agency scale.

---

This is where the science begins to touch philosophy.

Kashmir Śaivism describes manifestation as the contraction of universal capacities into limited centers of experience.

Infinite agency becomes *kalā*, restricted power.

Infinite knowledge becomes *vidyā*, local knowing.

Universal consciousness appears as countless finite subjects, each acting from a bounded perspective.

The resemblance to scale-free agency is striking.

Cells possess limited competencies.

Tissues integrate them into larger goals.

Organisms integrate tissues into behavior.

Communities and ecosystems may form higher-order patterns.

But resemblance is not identity.

Levin’s experiments do not demonstrate Śiva.

Tantric metaphysics does not predict specific ion-channel interventions.

The bridge must preserve the difference between empirical model and ontological interpretation.

---

Still, both frameworks resist one assumption:

That agency belongs only to the familiar human ego.

For Śaivism, the ego is a contracted expression of a more pervasive power of manifestation and self-relation.

For basal cognition, human intelligence is one evolved point on a spectrum of problem-solving systems.

The first begins from consciousness as fundamental.

The second studies observable competencies in living systems without requiring a final theory of consciousness.

One moves downward from metaphysics.

The other moves outward from experiments.

They meet at the question of where goals live.

---

When a planarian fragment regenerates, no single cell contains the whole target in the obvious way a central architect contains a building plan.

The goal is distributed across interactions.

Cells exchange signals.

Local states stabilize larger patterns.

The collective detects anatomical error and coordinates repair.

This challenges the habit of locating intelligence in a privileged point.

We search for the controller.

The brain.

The gene.

The command center.

But some systems govern through relationships rather than rulers.

The pattern controls by being enacted across the network.

---

Pratyabhijñā philosophy also treats identity as relationally manifested.

Consciousness becomes subject, object, and the act connecting them. A self is not an isolated substance sealed behind perception. It is a center produced within a field of manifestation.

At the human level, “I” feels singular.

At the cellular level, the body contains trillions of living agents with partial information and local priorities.

What makes them one organism?

Not genetic sameness alone; many cells share genomes while differentiating radically. Not spatial contact alone; tumors also inhabit the body while defecting from its larger goals.

Organismic unity is an achievement.

A political order of cells.

---

Cancer becomes especially revealing in this vocabulary.

Cancer cells are not simply dead machinery malfunctioning.

They are living cells pursuing local goals—proliferation, survival—while losing integration with the larger anatomical collective.

The scale of the self shrinks.

A cell stops participating adequately in the body’s long-range constraints and behaves as though its local lineage were the whole organism.

Levin has proposed that bioelectric communication may be involved in maintaining or restoring such integration.

This is not a moral claim about disease.

It is a systems claim:

Pathology can emerge when competent subunits abandon the goals of the larger whole.

---

Kashmir Śaivism describes bondage through a comparable contraction of identity.

The individual says:

I am only this body.

My knowledge ends here.

My good is separate from yours.

The universal field appears as a finite center that no longer recognizes the larger consciousness through which it exists.

Again, the comparison is structural, not causal.

Cancer is not spiritual egoism.

Spiritual realization is not a medical treatment.

But both models reveal how narrowing the boundary of the self changes behavior.

What counts as “me” determines what counts as error, threat, repair, and success.

---

This may be the most important frontier question:

How are the boundaries of an agent constructed?

A cell’s membrane creates one boundary.

Electrical coupling can join cells into larger computational networks.

Nervous systems bind sensory and motor processes into organism-level control.

Language and culture extend human goals across generations.

A self is not only found.

It is built.

And what is built may be widened, narrowed, damaged, or reorganized.

---

The Tantric concept of *saṃkoca* means contraction.

Universal power does not disappear; it becomes local.

Imagine a hand closing into a fist. The fingers remain, but their possible movements are compressed into one gesture.

A biological agent also emerges through constraint.

A membrane limits exchange.

A body plan restricts possible growth.

A nervous system selects relevant signals.

A goal excludes most possible states in favor of a narrow region of success.

Agency requires limitation.

A system that equally pursued every possible future would pursue nothing.

---

This suggests a surprising reversal.

Limitation is not the opposite of intelligence.

It is one of intelligence’s conditions.

To solve a problem, the system must distinguish preferred from nonpreferred states. It must care, in at least a minimal functional sense, about some outcomes more than others.

A planarian fragment “prefers” restoration only as shorthand for robust goal-directed behavior.

A human preference includes felt value.

The two should not be collapsed.

Yet both require asymmetry.

This state, not that one.

A head here.

A tail there.

Integrity rather than indefinite growth.

---

Michael Levin often uses the language of morphospace: an abstract space of possible anatomical configurations.

Cells and tissues navigate this space during development and regeneration. Injury moves the body away from its target region. Repair processes reduce the distance.

The image resembles movement through physical space, but the axes are shape variables.

Number of limbs.

Polarity.

Proportion.

Organ placement.

The body is not only located in a room.

It occupies a position in a space of possible forms.

Morphogenesis is navigation.

---

Tantric cosmology also imagines manifestation as movement through structured possibility.

The tattvas map progressive contraction from pure consciousness toward mind, senses, and material elements. They are not anatomical coordinates, but they describe how undivided capacity becomes increasingly specific form.

Both systems therefore require a landscape that is not ordinary physical geography.

Morphospace for biological form.

Tattvic space for modes of manifestation.

The analogy is useful because both treat visible matter as the endpoint of an organized process crossing hidden dimensions of possibility.

The analogy breaks because morphospace is a scientific abstraction used to model measurable forms, while tattvas are elements of a metaphysical cosmology.

Do not confuse map types.

---

Where does the target come from?

In biology, several answers may coexist.

Evolution shapes regulatory systems that reliably construct viable forms. Genetics constrains possible outcomes. Physics influences self-organization. Bioelectric networks store and process patterning information. Environmental feedback modifies development.

No single “little self” inside the organism needs to imagine the completed body.

Goal-directedness can emerge from the organization.

In Kashmir Śaivism, form ultimately arises through *svātantrya*, the freedom of consciousness to manifest its own possibilities.

The target is not externally imposed upon dead matter.

It belongs to the creative power of manifestation itself.

This is a metaphysical answer to a question science operationalizes without settling ontologically.

---

The temptation is to use Levin’s work as proof of ancient idealism.

That would weaken both.

Bioelectric pattern memory does not establish that consciousness is fundamental. Distributed error correction does not show that cells possess humanlike awareness. Scientific terms such as memory and goal can be used functionally without resolving subjective experience.

Conversely, Śaiva philosophy is not merely an early intuition of systems biology. Its central concern is liberation through recognition, not experimental control of regeneration.

A good bridge carries traffic while preserving the river beneath it.

---

What the science genuinely gives philosophy is pressure.

It makes the human monopoly on intelligence harder to defend.

It shows that memory-like dynamics can occur in non-neural tissue.

It reveals bodies as collective systems negotiating large-scale form.

It shifts the question from “Does this system have a brain like ours?” to “What problems can this system solve, across what scale and with what degree of flexibility?”

The world becomes populated by competencies.

Not necessarily conscious minds everywhere.

But organized agencies in unexpected places.

---

What the philosophy gives science is a vocabulary of scale and contraction.

A reminder that the observer’s categories may be biased toward one familiar center.

A question about whether increasingly large goal horizons change not only behavior but subjectivity.

A model in which individuality is real without being absolute.

These are not experimental results.

They are conceptual provocations.

Their value depends on whether they generate clearer questions rather than metaphysical shortcuts.

---

Now return to the planarian.

A fragment lies where a whole animal once moved.

Cells encounter injury signals.

Electrical patterns shift.

Genes change expression.

Stem cells proliferate.

Tissues reorganize.

A head appears where a head is needed.

Growth stops when proportion is restored.

The process does not look like a blueprint being mechanically printed.

It looks like a collective correcting its position in a space of possible bodies.

---

Your own body is performing quieter versions of this continuously.

Wounds close.

Cells die and are replaced.

Organs preserve structure despite molecular turnover.

Metabolism repairs deviations.

The body you call one thing is a negotiated persistence across change.

You do not consciously direct most of it.

Yet it belongs to the agency through which you exist.

The “you” solving the problem of your body is larger than the voice in your head.

---

Kashmir Śaivism would push further.

The consciousness you call yours is also larger than the voice in your head.

The ego is one local report issued by a deeper field of manifestation.

Recognition occurs when the contracted center discovers that its power of knowing and acting did not originate inside the boundary it defended.

The cells do not become human.

The human does not become a cell colony and nothing more.

But the neat hierarchy fractures.

Agency is layered.

Identity is nested.

The whole acts through parts that possess their own limited goals.

---

Cut a planarian into pieces.

The animal becomes a question distributed across wounds.

What shape belongs here?

The cells answer together.

Cut a human life through loss, transition, or injury, and another target becomes uncertain.

What form should return?

Biology can describe some repair mechanisms.

Philosophy asks what counts as wholeness.

The answer may not be a return to the previous shape.

Living systems preserve identity partly by changing it.

---

Your cells may be solving a problem called you.

Not your biography.

Not your ambitions.

The anatomical and physiological coherence that makes biography possible.

And the person you call “I” may be solving another problem inside a still larger field:

How can a bounded perspective recognize the powers that exceed its boundary without losing the reality of its own form?

The worm regenerates by remembering a body.

Recognition philosophy asks whether consciousness liberates itself by remembering the field.

Two different memories.

Two different kinds of evidence.

One shared disturbance:

The whole may be present in its parts not as a tiny picture, but as a goal they learn to enact together.
