# pleasure is what happens when experience stops fighting itself

A chord can contain tension without becoming unpleasant.

A tragedy can contain grief without making the audience flee.

A difficult meditation can contain pain while the whole field feels strangely peaceful.

The local contents are not enough to predict the value of the experience.

Something about the organization of the whole changes what every part becomes.

The Qualia Research Institute calls this problem valence.

Kashmir Śaivism and Abhinavagupta’s aesthetics call it *rasa*, *sāmarasya*, and *camatkāra*.

Both begin from the same impossible fact:

pleasure is not simply the presence of pleasant ingredients.

Pleasure is what happens when experience stops fighting itself.

---

QRI’s Symmetry Theory of Valence begins with two commitments.

First, **Qualia Formalism**: for every conscious experience, there is some mathematical object whose structure is isomorphic to the structure of that experience.

Second, **Valence Realism**: pleasure and suffering are real features of consciousness, not merely verbal reports or behavioural preferences.

If both are true, then valence should correspond to a structural property of the mathematical object.

The proposed property is symmetry.

More symmetrical phenomenal structures should feel better.

Broken, frustrated, dissonant, or anti-symmetrical structures should feel worse.

This is a bold hypothesis, not an established law.

---

The intuition is easy to feel through sound.

Two tones interact.

Their partials reinforce or interfere.

Some relations generate rough beating.

Others produce periodic coincidence.

Consonant intervals are often judged more pleasant than strongly dissonant ones, and auditory-cortex studies detect different responses before reflective judgement is complete.

Yet consonance is not one thing.

Roughness, harmonicity, familiarity, musical syntax, expectation, training, and culture all contribute.

A minor chord is not unpleasant merely because its frequency relations differ from a major chord.

A suspension can be beautiful because tension is understood as part of a larger phrase.

The nervous system evaluates structure across several scales.

---

This creates the first difficulty for STV.

What exactly is the relevant symmetry?

Visual reflection?

Temporal repetition?

Harmonic coincidence?

Spatial regularity?

Network automorphism?

Phase synchrony?

Compressibility under transformations?

A perfectly repeated tone can become unbearable.

A symmetrical face can appear lifeless.

A complex improvised passage can be intensely pleasurable despite lacking obvious periodicity.

The theory cannot mean that more visible regularity always equals more pleasure.

The relevant symmetry must belong to the full phenomenal object, not merely to the stimulus.

That object includes attention, prediction, bodily state, memory, temporal context, and the way local features bind into a whole.

---

QRI’s CDNS proposal—consonance, dissonance and noise analysis—tries to move toward a neural version.

The idea is that valence may depend partly on the degree to which neural patterns resonate coherently rather than frustrate one another.

Consonance would support compatible standing-wave structures.

Dissonance would create conflict among modes.

Noise would reduce organized binding.

This remains a research programme.

No accepted instrument currently reads a brain pattern and outputs an accurate universal pleasure score.

But the proposal gives valence a potentially measurable geometry.

Suffering may be more than “negative information.”

It may involve the way information is forced to coexist.

---

Abhinavagupta’s aesthetic theory begins at the exact point where a simple stimulus theory fails.

A dramatic scene depicts grief.

The spectator experiences grief-like affect.

Yet the experience can be relished.

Why does negative content become aesthetic pleasure?

The answer is not that the content secretly becomes happy.

The grief is **universalized**.

It is released from the spectator’s immediate practical ownership.

This is not my bereavement requiring action now.

It is grief disclosed as a human possibility, supported by performance, rhythm, distance, recognition, and shared form.

The affect remains.

The contraction around “me” and “mine” loosens.

The local pain enters a larger organization in which it can be tasted without overwhelming the whole.

---

This is *rasa*.

The word means flavour, juice, aesthetic relish.

An emotion ordinarily drives action.

Fear demands escape.

Desire demands acquisition.

Grief circles an absence.

In aesthetic experience, the action-demand is suspended while the affective form remains vivid.

The emotion becomes available for contemplation and enjoyment.

The spectator does not become numb.

The affect becomes more fully itself because it is no longer trapped inside one urgent autobiographical problem.

Rasa is negative or positive emotion transformed by the geometry of ownership.

---

This suggests a refinement of the Symmetry Theory of Valence.

Pleasure may not correspond to symmetry in the sense of repetition.

It may correspond to **global compatibility**.

Can the differentiated components of experience coexist without one part continually treating another as an intolerable error?

In tragedy, local grief is compatible with the global aesthetic field.

In music, dissonance is compatible with the phrase that gives it direction.

In meditation, pain may remain local while resistance relaxes around it.

In suffering, by contrast, one component can dominate the whole:

this must stop;

this should not be happening;

I cannot contain this;

the future has collapsed around this state.

The experience begins fighting its own existence.

---

The Tantric term *sāmarasya* points toward this larger compatibility.

It can mean equal flavour, harmony, or a state in which opposed powers are reconciled without losing distinction.

Śiva and Śakti are not harmonized by becoming identical in function.

Śiva signifies illumination.

Śakti signifies reflexivity and power.

Stillness and activity remain different.

Their difference no longer produces metaphysical conflict.

The whole can contain both.

This is not static symmetry.

It is difference that no longer fractures the field.

---

A similar structure appears in Abhinavagupta’s *camatkāra*—astonished relish or self-enjoying wonder.

The experience does not terminate in possession of an object.

Awareness savours its own power of manifestation through the object.

Beauty is not located entirely in the artwork.

It emerges when the artwork, affect, memory, attention, and awareness form a self-revealing circuit.

The object remains distinct.

The subject remains distinct.

The relation becomes enjoyable because neither side is experienced as alien to the field in which both appear.

The pleasure is reflexive.

Consciousness enjoys its capacity to become this form.

---

This helps explain why symmetry can be pleasant without being sufficient.

Symmetry reduces conflict under transformation.

A pattern remains recognizable when rotated, reflected, or repeated.

The system can encode more with fewer rules.

Prediction becomes easier.

But absolute predictability can become empty.

Pleasure often requires symmetry plus difference.

Theme and variation.

Tension and release.

Centre and ornament.

A perfectly uniform field contains no contrast to organize.

A completely random field contains no relation to sustain.

Aesthetic life occupies the middle where variation remains intelligible to the whole.

---

Recent formal work on qualia and structure approaches this issue from another side.

Xerxes Arsiwalla models phenomenal content through intrinsic meaning and relations rather than treating qualia as isolated atoms.

Pedro Resende proposes a topological space of qualia with additional structures for time and abstraction.

Ryan Williams develops measures of fidelity between structural and qualitative domains and asks under which conditions evolution could reliably transmit qualitative effects through physical structures.

None of these papers establishes STV.

They make its problem more exact.

If valence depends on structure, a theory must explain which structural relations preserve qualitative meaning across different brains, bodies, and contexts.

---

The evolutionary question is severe.

Pleasure often guides adaptive behaviour.

Food tastes good.

Injury hurts.

Social acceptance comforts.

But evolution selects behaviour and reproduction through physical mechanisms.

For qualitative valence to guide selection reliably, changes in physical structure must map consistently onto changes in felt value.

If the mapping were arbitrary, selection could not stabilize useful affective guidance.

Williams’s work formalizes constraints on this structure-quality transmission.

The implication is not that evolution proves pleasure is symmetry.

It is that any structural theory of valence must explain why the mapping remains stable enough for organisms to learn from feeling.

---

Tantric theory makes a different claim.

Valence is not created by evolution from qualitatively neutral matter.

At the deepest level, consciousness contains an inherent self-relish.

Contraction produces desire, lack, aversion, and pain.

Pleasure often appears when contraction temporarily relaxes and awareness recovers a degree of its own fullness.

This is why finite objects disappoint.

They are asked to restore an unbounded condition through bounded possession.

The object may produce relief.

The structure of lack soon returns.

From this perspective, positive valence is not symmetry added to consciousness.

It is consciousness encountering less internal obstruction to its own reflexivity.

---

Science cannot currently test this metaphysical claim.

It can test whether reports of pleasantness correlate with neural synchrony, harmonic organization, low prediction conflict, or other candidate variables.

It can compare music, pain, meditation, anaesthesia, and mood.

It can falsify simple versions of STV.

It cannot infer universal consciousness merely because global neural patterns predict valence.

The bridge is philosophical:

STV asks whether the mathematical whole determines pleasure.

Abhinavagupta asks whether pleasure reveals how completely the whole recognizes itself.

---

The lived consequence is practical.

Trying to remove every unpleasant component may not create well-being.

A life can become narrower, more defended, and more fragile while successfully avoiding discomfort.

Another strategy changes the relation among components.

Pain can be held inside safety.

Grief can be held inside meaning.

Uncertainty can be held inside curiosity.

Desire can be held without becoming command.

The local signal remains negative.

The global field stops treating its existence as catastrophic.

Valence changes through inclusion rather than deletion.

---

This does not romanticize suffering.

Severe pain, danger, and illness require protection and care.

No philosophical reframing makes every state beneficial.

The point is narrower.

The value of an experience cannot always be calculated by adding the valence of isolated parts.

The whole changes the parts.

A dissonance inside music differs from unstructured noise.

Grief inside rasa differs from personal devastation.

Pain inside equanimity differs from pain multiplied by panic.

Global organization is causally and phenomenologically real.

---

A chord can contain tension without becoming unpleasant.

A tragedy can contain grief and become beautiful.

The Symmetry Theory of Valence proposes that pleasure corresponds to structural symmetry.

Abhinavagupta’s aesthetics suggests the deeper criterion may be *sāmarasya*: differentiated contents integrated without unresolved conflict and relished through reflexive awareness.

Pleasure is what happens when experience stops fighting itself.

Not when every difference disappears.

When difference discovers a whole large enough to let it remain.
