# THE UNFOLDING CONFIGURATIONS OF THE GODDESSES OF CONSCIOUSNESS
## 10-Minute Deep-Dive Video Essay — Tantrāloka Vol. 2, Chapter 3, Verses 265cd–267

---

### Core Teaching

Having reached the consummation of the violent digestion — where the deities of consciousness feed upon the universe turned to nectar and rest in Bhairava — one may ask: if these goddesses rest in the supreme nature where nothing is separate, how can they possess a twelve-fold or manifold nature? Abhinavagupta answers: although their nature is ultimately one and undivided (*abheda*), it appears as many through the diversity of functions (*kṛtya*), operations (*kriyā*), names (*nāma*), and modes of worship (*upāsā*). The goddesses of consciousness — identified as the twelve Kālīs presiding over the senses — merge together (*āvāpa*) and unfold (*udvāpa*) in configurations from one to countless, creating the infinite diversity of cognitive experience while never ceasing to be the one consciousness. This is the Krama teaching of the dynamic self-differentiation of the absolute.

**Line references:** TĀ 3/265cd–267; Dyczkowski translation pp. 390–394, lines 28227–28410.

### Unity in Multiplicity (Verses 265cd–266ab)

**Line references:** 28227–28268.

**Sanskrit:**
```
evaṁ kṛtyakriyāveśān nāmopāsābahutvataḥ || 265 ||
āsāṁ bahuvidhaṁ rūpam abhede 'py avabhāsate |
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:** "Although their nature manifests in unity (*abheda*) in this way, it is of many kinds, for many are their names and manner of worship (*upāsā*), due to their functions and operations."

**Explanation:** The word *evaṁ* (in this way) refers back to the preceding description of the goddesses resting in Bhairava. Despite that unity, experience is manifestly diverse. Abhinavagupta accounts for this diversity through four factors: *kṛtya* (function — perceiving form, sound, etc.), *kriyā* (operation — the activity of the senses), *nāma* (name — "deity of sight," "deity of hearing," etc.), and *upāsā* (worship — the meditative merger of each sense's activity into consciousness).

Jayaratha explains: "The point of saying that 'although their nature manifests in unity, it is of many kinds' is to indicate that their diverse form is purely conceptual (*kālpanika*) and not ultimately real." The diversity is real at the phenomenal level but does not compromise the nondual nature of consciousness.

### The Twelve Kālīs as the Goddesses of the Senses

**Line references:** 28238–28268.

The twelve Kālīs, as we see in detail in Chapter Four, are the goddesses of the twelve phases of the universal activity (*sāmānyaspanda*) of consciousness. They correspond to emanation, persistence, withdrawal, and their ineffable oneness, in the domains of the object, means of knowledge, and the subject. In this context, they are identified with the twelve goddesses of the senses:

- **Five senses of knowledge (*jñānendriya*):** hearing, taste, sight, smell, touch
- **Five senses of action (*karmendriya*):** hands, feet, speech, anus, genitals
- **Two inner organs:** mind (*manas*) and intellect (*buddhi*)

The "worship" (*upāsā*) of these goddesses is the merger (*vilāpana*) of their activity into consciousness. For each sense, the act of perception — seeing form, hearing sound, etc. — becomes an offering. The goddess of that sense, satisfied, rests back in Bhairava.

### The Four-Fold and Five-Fold Sequences of Perception

**Line references:** 28269–28352.

Jayaratha introduces the Krama analysis of the perceptual process, which follows a sequence of consciousness (*saṁvitkrama*). Two main formats are found in Krama sources:

**The Four-Fold Sequence (*kramacatuṣṭaya*):**
1. **Udyoga** (initial exertion) — the stirring within the absolute to manifest as the universe of finite experience
2. **Avabhāsana** (manifestation) — the appearing of that content within consciousness
3. **Carvaṇa** (relishing) — the reflective awareness of that appearing, the aesthetic delight of recognition
4. **Vilāpana** (merger) — the withdrawal back into the absolute, also called the Devouring of Time (*kālagrāsa*)

**The Five-Fold Sequence:**
1. **Ābhāsana** (manifestation) — the unfolding of the powers of the senses in the act of perception
2. **Rakti** (attachment) — persistence of the emitted manifestation, the subject coloured by the object
3. **Vimarśana** (reflection) — withdrawal, the subject turning back to rest within itself
4. **Bījāvasthāpana** (sowing the seed) — deposition of a residue that may seed future conditioned states
5. **Vilāpana** (merger) — final dissolution, the grace that marks the cessation of limiting adjuncts

In the case of the perfected yogi, the process ends at the third phase (reflection), contained in the consciousness of Śiva's self-revelation. For most, a residue remains as the seed for future conditioned consciousness — a doubt that assumes the form of the thought constructs that veil consciousness.

### Āvāpa and Udvāpa: Merging and Unfolding (Verses 266cd–267)

**Line references:** 28353–28387.

**Sanskrit:**
```
āsām eva ca devīnām āvāpodvāpayogataḥ || 266 ||
ekadvitricatuṣpañcaṣaṭsaptāsṭanavottaraiḥ |
rudrārkānyakalāsenāprabhṛtirbhedaviṣṭaraḥ || 267 ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:** "Again, according to the way in which (these) goddesses merge together (*āvāpa*) and unfold (*udvāpa*), they become extensively diverse (by combining in groups of) one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, eight, nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, sixteen, eighteen and so on (infinitely)."

**Explanation:** If a doubt arises that the twelve-fold nature should be fixed because only twelve functions exist, Abhinavagupta replies: the goddesses are not static categories but dynamic powers that *merge* (āvāpa — gathering together, contraction) and *unfold* (udvāpa — expansion, blooming) freely, like a lotus opening and closing with the sun.

By these two operations — contraction and expansion — every possible configuration of energy arises. A single goddess may preside over an entire cognitive act; two may combine; three, four, five, up to the twelve, and then combining further into thirteen (twelve plus one principle), sixteen (twelve plus the four phases), eighteen, and so on infinitely. The *bhedaviṣṭaraḥ* (extensive diversity) is the entire tapestry of cosmic manifestation, woven from the warp and weft of *āvāpa* and *udvāpa*.

Jayaratha notes: "By virtue of their own freedom, they constantly contract and expand freely like a lotus that opens and closes as the sun rises and sets." This image beautifully captures the spontaneous, aesthetic nature of cosmic manifestation — not a mechanical emanation but a living, pulsing dance of the goddesses of consciousness.

### Philosophical Significance

1. **Nondual Diversity**: The multiplicity of goddesses does not contradict the nonduality of consciousness. As Jayaratha stresses, the diverse form is "purely conceptual" (*kālpanika*) — functional distinctions within the one light of awareness, not ontological separations.

2. **The Senses as Goddesses**: The identification of the twelve Kālīs with the twelve senses (ten outer, two inner) interiorizes the entire Krama cosmology. The Wheel of Energies is not a distant cosmic diagram but the immediate structure of every act of perception. One's own body and senses are the temple and the deities.

3. **Āvāpa-Udvāpa as the Rhythm of Reality**: The paired operations of merging and unfolding are the fundamental rhythm of consciousness — the systolic and diastolic pulse of the absolute. Everything from a single perception to the entire cosmic cycle follows this pattern of contraction and expansion.

4. **Krama-Trika Synthesis**: By integrating the Krama analysis of perceptual phases (four-fold and five-fold sequences) into the Trika framework of the twelve Kālīs, Abhinavagupta demonstrates that the Krama system is not a separate teaching but the detailed elaboration of the Trika's own inner logic.

5. **The Freedom of the Goddesses**: The phrase "by virtue of their own freedom" (*svātantrya*) is crucial. The configurations arise not by necessity but through the spontaneous play (*līlā*) of the goddesses — the aesthetic freedom of consciousness to manifest in countless forms while remaining ever one.

---

### Jayaratha's Commentary Insights

Jayaratha expands on the twelve-fold function of the senses: consciousness flows through the senses that it itself generates as its instruments of perception. Consequently, the same phases operate through each of the senses in every act of perception. The five-fold sequence is described in detail, particularly the delicate moment of *bījāvasthāpana* (sowing the seed) where, in most cases, a residue remains that functions as the seed for future conditioned states. This residue assumes the form of doubt or uncertainty about the nature and consequence of further activity, which then spontaneously takes the form of the thought constructs that veil consciousness.

He clarifies the relationship between the *āvāpa* and *udvāpa* and the earlier teaching about wheels of energy ranging from one to sixty-four to a thousand spokes (verses 254cd–257ab). The twelve are fundamental, but by merging and unfolding they generate every possible configuration — answering the potential objection that a purely twelve-fold system would be too limited to account for the infinite diversity of experience.
