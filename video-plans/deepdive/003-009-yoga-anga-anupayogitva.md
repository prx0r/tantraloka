# THE USELESSNESS OF THE ANCILLARIES OF YOGA (YOGĀṄGĀNUPAYOGITVA)
## 10-Minute Deep-Dive Video Essay — Tantrāloka Vol. 3, Chapter 4, Verses 86–109ab

---

### Core Teaching

In the context of Śāktopāya, Abhinavagupta makes a radical claim: the only true ancillary of Yoga is Sound Reasoning (*sattarka*). The conventional eight limbs of Yoga — yama, niyama, āsana, prāṇāyāma, pratyāhāra, dhāraṇā, dhyāna, samādhi — are of no use if they do not contribute to the development of liberating insight. This is not a rejection of practice but a radical reorientation: from the perspective of the highest nondual realization (*Anuttara*), "the ancillaries of Yoga are of no use as a means to anything" because "the form of Anuttara is that it has no form, being devoid of all defining limitations." Breath control and postures pertain to the body, which is already recognized as a manifestation of consciousness — what need is there to discipline what one already knows as oneself?

**Line references:** TĀ 4/86–109ab; Dyczkowski translation pp. 101–129, lines 7668–8700.

---

### Key Verses

#### Verse 86 — Sound Reasoning as the Sole Ancillary

**Sanskrit:**
```
evaṁ yogāṅgam iyati tarka eva na cāparam |
antarantaḥ parāmarśapāṭavātiśayāya saḥ || 86 ||
```
**Dyczkowski Translation (lines 7673–7737):** "In this way, (there is ultimately only one) ancillary of Yoga (*yogāṅga*) which is (established to be) such, namely, discriminative reasoning (*tarka*), and none other, bestowing as it does an ever more internal and excellent capacity for reflective awareness."

Jayaratha explains: This is quoting MV 17/18ab, which declares that "Sound intuitive Reasoning is the most excellent ancillary of Yoga." Abhinava "goes a step further by saying that it is the *only* one" (lines 7707–7711). The development of insight is the path to realization; the practice of the limbs of Yoga are "of no use if they do not contribute to that development."

---

#### Verses 105–108 (from MVV 2/105–108) — The Uselessness of All Means

Quoted at lines 7679–7705:

"For one who is well established in the expansion (of consciousness) alone, Māyā is without root. In this way, what is the use of speaking much in this doctrine called Anuttara? In actual fact, the ancillaries of Yoga are of no use as a means to anything. The form (*svarūpa*) of Anuttara is that it has no form because it is devoid of all defining limitations. Although a means, it is no means (*anupāya*), because all that takes place is that the yogi's states that are unfit for realization are blocked. This body of the disembodied one is devoid of exhalations and inhalations. In this way, Anuttara makes the Self cross over the ocean of saṁsāra, even though the mind plunges into the aesthetic delight of the objects of sense."

---

### The Critique of Breath Control (Prāṇāyāma)

Jayaratha (lines 7804–7826) presents the argument: "How can that which is blocked all around within consciousness, the form of which is the vital breath and the intellect, move inwardly within this consciousness?" The practice of breath control operates within the sphere of the body-mind complex, which is itself a manifestation of consciousness. To use it to attain consciousness is like using a wave to find the ocean — the wave is already the ocean.

The MVV (2/4–7, lines 7804–7826) is quoted: "Practice is something that is repeated again and again, and that too is only possible because there are differences between moments of time and locations in space. Those meditations which seek to discern reality within Bhairava's nature, which is the compact uninterrupted light of consciousness, are well established within it by resting in the consciousness which has shone forth just once. Thus the limb of Yoga which is supreme and serves to help all others — sattarka — is the means by which this supreme reality is realized."

---

### The Epistemological Argument (Verses 88–96)

Abhinavagupta anticipates objections (lines 7828–7900): "What faith could one conceive to have in reasoning which has no sound basis?" The response is devastating: all means of knowledge — direct perception, inference, scripture — are subject to mutual contradiction and infinite regress. Yet scripture itself declares that "sound reasoning" is the valid means to liberation. The only stable ground is the self-luminous consciousness that is its own authority.

---

### The Fifth Vow — Sattarka as the True Yoga

Jayaratha summarizes (lines 8000–8100): The practice of Śāktopāya does not require the eight limbs of Patañjali's Yoga. Instead, it requires the single "limb" of *sattarka* — the discerning insight that gradually purifies thought until consciousness shines in its pristine clarity. This is the "fifth vow" of the Kaula tradition: the vow to abide in the realization of one's true nature.

---

### The Continuity Argument

Swami Lakshmanjoo (lines 7650–7658) explains: "There is always something more to realize. Like undressing a woman, embracing her and having sex with her, there is always more. It is not 'useless' as the Buddhists think, who consider that once an object has been perceived there is no further means to cognition. Nor is it an infinite regress, because in realizing God Consciousness you realize more and more from the Master, from scripture and from what you can experience."

---

### Philosophical Significance

1. **The Inward Turn**: The ancillaries of Yoga are not rejected as worthless but are subordinated to their purpose: the development of insight. When insight is present, they are unnecessary; when absent, they are insufficient.

2. **The Principle of Anupāya**: The highest teaching is "No Means" — when consciousness recognizes itself as all that is, no practice is needed. The ancillaries of Yoga belong to the sphere of the Individual Means (*āṇavopāya*), which is the lowest category.

3. **Sattarka Replaces All Limbs**: The eight limbs are collapsed into one: discerning insight. This is not reduction but distillation — the essence of all yogic practice is the purification of thought through correct understanding.

4. **The Body as Consciousness**: If all is consciousness, the body and its practices are already within consciousness. Disciplining the body to realize consciousness is like using a lamp to find the sun — the sun is its own light.
