# 340 — The Seed and the Sprout: Succession and Continuity
**Tantrāloka 9.13–17ab** | Chapter 9: Kāryakāraṇa (Causality)

## Hook
Does the seed die so the sprout can live? The relationship between seed and sprout is the classic Indian test case for any theory of causality.

## Core Teaching
The seed does not cease to exist when the sprout arises. Rather, the universal nature (*tattva*) of "seed-ness" continues in the sprout. Causality is not replacement but manifestation of what was already implicit.

## The Problem
If the seed is the cause:
- Either the seed continues to exist when the sprout appears (then we have two things, not cause-effect)
- Or the seed ceases to exist (then nothing connects cause to effect)
- Or both exist simultaneously (but they appear sequentially)
- Or neither exists (absurd)

All options fail under Buddhist assumptions.

## Abhinavagupta's Solution
The seed and sprout share a common reality — a *tattva* — which is a mode of consciousness. The apparent succession is the unfolding of that reality across time, guided by Śiva's will. The cause-effect relation is:

1. **Not replacement** — the same reality continues
2. **Not identity** — the forms differ
3. **Manifestation** — consciousness manifests successively as seed-form and sprout-form

## The Threefold Manifestation (Recap from 9.8–10)
- **Internal** — the seed's reality is always present in consciousness
- **Objective** — it appears to mind as "seed"
- **External** — it appears to senses as "sprout"

## Why This Matters
The seed-sprout argument is the empirical demonstration of the *satkāryavāda* (effect pre-exists in cause) reframed in non-dual terms. Unlike the Sāṁkhya version (where *prakṛti* transforms), here the transformation is within consciousness.

## Jayaratha's Note
The seed's "causal capacity" is nothing other than the power of consciousness to manifest forms in succession. There is no mysterious "power" in the seed itself.

## Connection to Sāmagrī
The seed-sprout continuity argument logically unfolds into the *sāmagrī* (aggregate of causes) discussion: causality requires the convergence of multiple factors held together by the single agent, Śiva.
