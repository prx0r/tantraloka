# ŚIVA TATTVA — Pure I-Consciousness
## 10-Minute Deep-Dive Video Essay

---

### Core Teaching

The Śiva tattva is the first and thirty-sixth principle (ṣaṭtriṃśa tattva), the ultimate ontological ground of the entire hierarchy of reality. It is pure unqualified consciousness (śuddha saṃvit) defined as *prakāśa* — the self-luminous Light of consciousness that requires no external illumination — inseparable from *vimarśa* — its inherent reflective awareness by which it knows itself as "I" (*ahaṁvimarśa*). Unlike all lower principles, the Śiva tattva is *sarvāvacchedaśūnya* — utterly devoid of all limitation — and functions as the substrate in which the remaining thirty-five tattvas appear as reflections (*pratibimba*) within the mirror of absolute I-consciousness. It is the *svatantra* (absolutely free) reality that Abhinavagupta identifies as *prakāśarūpa śivatattva* — the Śiva principle whose very essence is Light (Tantrasāra, 3rd Āhnika). This principle is not a "thing" among things but the very subjectivity that constitutes all experience, the *ahan* (I) that is the heart (*hṛdaya*) of all reality.

### Key Verses from Tantrāloka

#### Verse 1.52 — The Supreme Reality of the Object is Śiva as Light

**Line:** ~14131 (Vol. 1, Chapter One)

**Sanskrit:**
```
jñeyasya hi paraṁ tattvaṁ yaḥ prakāśātmakaḥ śivaḥ |
na hy aprakāśarūpasya prākāśyaṁ vastutāpi vā || 52 ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"The supreme reality (paraṁ tattvam) of the object (of perception) is Śiva Who is the Light (of consciousness), for that which is not Light can neither be illumined (i.e. be made manifest) nor even have (any) existence (vastutā)."

**Explanation:**
This foundational verse establishes that the *ultimate* reality (paraṁ tattva) of every object of perception is Śiva himself, defined as *prakāśātmaka* — one whose very self (*ātman*) is Light. Dyczkowski notes (note 545) that the second line is quoted by Maheśvarānanda in the Mahārthamañjarī to conclude a passage arguing that all entities, from potters to pots, are ultimately nothing but the Light of consciousness. The verse declares an identity: the manifestness (*prakāśamānatā*) of any object is not an accident but its very being. If something were not Light (i.e., not consciousness), it could neither appear nor exist. This is the non-dualist rejoinder to the Sāṁkhya dualist position that objects are independently real and merely *illumined* by an external consciousness.

---

#### Verse 1.53 — Non-Existence Also is Within Consciousness

**Line:** ~14377 (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit:**
```
avastutāpi bhāvānāṁ camatkāraikagocarā |
yat kuḍyasadṛśī neyaṁ dhīr avastv etad ity api || 53 ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"The non-existence (avastutā) of entities is also in one (and the same) field of the wonder (of consciousness as is their existence). The notion (dhī) 'this is non-existent (avastu)' is not similar to (an inert object such as) a wall."

**Explanation:**
Even the cognition of absence — "there is no pot here" — is a mode of consciousness. The very act of negating is a positive state of awareness, proving that consciousness underlies even the perception of non-being. Therefore, Śiva tattva as absolute consciousness cannot be negated, because the negator and the act of negation are themselves consciousness.

---

#### Verse 1.54 — The Light Shines Always, Requires No Proof

**Line:** ~14438 (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit:**
```
prakāśo nāma yaś cāyaṁ sarvadaiva prakāśate |
anapahnavanīyatvāt kiṁ tasmin mānakalpanaiḥ || 54 ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"Indeed, this Light (of consciousness) shines at all times; so, as it cannot be denied, what is the use (of applying) contrived means of knowledge to it? (All that needs be done is to recognise that the One Light exists and that it illumines the manifestation of all things.)"

**Explanation:**
The Light of consciousness is self-proving (*ādisiddha* — proved right from the start). It precedes all means of knowledge (*pramāṇa*). Dyczkowski explains (note 548): "The light of consciousness irrefutably precedes all knowledge." Any attempt to *prove* the existence of consciousness already presupposes it. This is the cornerstone of the Pratyabhijñā argument for the self-validity of consciousness.

---

#### Verse 1.55 — Consciousness is the Life of the Means of Knowledge Themselves

**Line:** ~14574 (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit:**
```
pramāṇāny api vastānāṁ jīvitaṁ yāni tanvate |
teṣām api paro jīvaḥ sa eva parameśvaraḥ || 55 ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"The (many) means of knowledge give life to all things (vastu) (because it is by means of them that they appear to the sentient subject), while the supreme living essence (jīva) of those (means) also is that same Supreme Lord (Himself Who is the true identity of the perceiver)."

**Explanation:**
Not only do objects depend on consciousness, but the very instruments of knowing (perception, inference, etc.) are themselves animated by the Light. The *pramāṇas* function only because the Supreme Lord — Śiva tattva — is their inner life (*jīva*). The resting place (*viśrāntiṣṭhāna*) of all cognitive acts is the reflective awareness of I-consciousness (*ahaṁvimarśa*).

---

#### Verse 1.59cd-60ab — Śiva is Ubiquitous, Eternal, Omniform

**Line:** ~15080+ (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit:**
```
anapekṣasya vaśino deśakālākṛtikramāḥ |
niyatā neti sa vibhur nityo viśvākṛtiḥ śivaḥ || 59cd-60ab ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"For one who, independent and the master (of all things, transcends them,) spatial, temporal and formal processes are not restricted (niyata) (by Karma and natural law), and so He is Śiva Who is ubiquitous, eternal and omniform."

**Explanation:**
Because Śiva tattva is independent (*anapekṣa*) and the master (*vaśin*), space, time, and form cannot limit it. Jayaratha's commentary explains: space cannot divide it (hence *vibhu* — ubiquitous), time cannot sequence it (hence *nitya* — eternal), and form cannot contain it (hence *viśvākṛti* — omniform). The Śiva tattva is not one object among others but the very medium in which all spatial, temporal, and formal relations appear.

---

#### Verse 1.60cd-61ab — Manifestation of the Sentient and Insentient

**Line:** ~15180+ (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit:**
```
vibhutvāt sarvago nityabhāvād ādyantavarjitaḥ |
viśvākṛtitvāc cidacittadvaicitryāvabhāsakaḥ || 60cd-61ab ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"By virtue of His ubiquity, He is all-pervading. As He is eternal, He is free of beginning and end. As He is omniform, He manifests the wonderful variety of things, both sentient and insentient."

**Explanation:**
The Śiva tattva is not merely a static ground but the dynamic power that manifests the entire range of reality — from sentient beings to insentient matter — as aspects of its own light. This directly refutes any notion of a passive Brahman.

### Commentary from Jayaratha (Viveka)

**On TĀ 1.52 (line ~14166):**
Jayaratha explains that verse 52 connects to verse 47 above and establishes that *prakāśa* is the innermost nature of all things. He cites the *Mālinīvijayavārttika* (p. 36) which argues: "The nature (of an entity) is ultimately the Light (of consciousness) as that which gives it life (and existence). This is understood (to be so) by both positive and negative concomitance. (Thus, if it were) otherwise, even if the aggregate of factors that make for perception, such as the sense of sight and the rest, are present, the (undesirable) consequence would be that they would not manifest."

**On the distinction of objects (line ~14258):**
Jayaratha quotes the *Mālinīvijayavārttika*: "The radiant flashing forth of the wonderful variety of all things is like the sprouts and flowers of the tree of the reflective awareness of one's own Self." The differences between things — blue vs. yellow, earth vs. water — arise through *vimarśa* (reflective awareness) within the undifferentiated Light. This differentiation culminates in a state of oneness (*tādātmya*).

**On Supreme Freedom (line ~14324):**
Jayaratha comments: "The freedom, previously described, that Paramaśiva possesses brings (all things) to rest in transcendence alone. For it is not proper that one's own innate nature, which is such and is within the empire of the wealth of supreme freedom that is both transcendent and immanent, be withdrawn. Rather, one's own innate nature is the state of withdrawal itself."

**On TĀ 1.54 (line ~14448):**
Jayaratha explains that a means of knowledge has as its object something new that has not been known before. But the Light of consciousness shines at all times and so cannot be a *new* object. Therefore, no *pramāṇa* can grasp it. It is *ādisiddha* — proved from the beginning.

**On the Self as Light (TĀv on 1.59cd-60ab, line ~15140+):**
Jayaratha glosses *anapekṣasya* as "free," and explains that space, time, and form cannot divide Śiva because they are themselves conditioned by thought constructs based on unity and diversity (*bhedābheda*). The Light of consciousness is not divided by space, not sequenced by time, not limited by form.

### Dyczkowski's Notes

"Perception of an object entails both presentation and representation. The former is called prakāśa and the latter vimarśa. They are two aspects of the activity of the one consciousness. The former is the light of the object that, manifesting, 'shines'. The latter is the cognitive response (vimarśana) of the perceiver, who seeks to ascertain the nature of what is appearing."

**On Ādisiddha — Proved from the Start (Vol. 1, note 548, line ~14488):**
"The light of consciousness irrefutably precedes all knowledge."

**On the Self-Proving Subject (Vol. 1, note 553, line ~14733):**
"The pure subjectivity of consciousness which escapes, because it 'precedes', objectification in this way, is therefore unconditioned, and so is the consciousness of Deity itself."

**On Anuttara as Śiva Tattva (Vol. 1, note 18, line ~1448):**
Abhinava explains Anuttara as "that which has nothing higher beyond it." All thirty-six principles, up to Śiva without Support, are such as they are according to the degree to which they penetrate into consciousness, which is the Supreme Bhairava. But supreme consciousness itself is perfectly full, the aesthetic relish of consciousness independent of all things.

**On the Thirty-sixth Principle (Vol. 1, line ~28926):**
"The thirty-sixth principle beyond that is Śiva, Who is pure transcendental oneness. This is the sphere of nonduality (abheda) which, after the Principle of the Self and Śakti, is the third, the Principle of Śiva."

"All the thirty-six principles, arranged in this sequence and groupings, form a causal chain such that the immediately preceding group is the cause of the succeeding one. As the higher principles generate the lower, the upper end of the chain is the ultimate uncaused cause of everything."

### Philosophical Context

#### How does Śiva tattva relate to Anuttara?

Anuttara (the Absolute, "none higher") is the supreme designation for the ultimate reality in Abhinava's Anuttara Trika system. The Śiva tattva is the first *manifestation* of Anuttara as the principle of pure I-consciousness. Anuttara as such is beyond even the Śiva tattva — it is the Inexplicable (*anākhya*), the 38th principle that transcends all. However, in practical terms, the Śiva tattva functions as the first principle from which the emanation of the tattvas begins. Abhinava identifies Anuttara with the Light of Śiva and the power of reflective awareness (PTv p. 19-28). As Dyczkowski notes (Vol. 1, line ~1104): "Anuttara is the ineffable oneness from which the original polarities of I-ness emerge."

In the Tantrasāra's third Āhnika (line 69), the *prakāśarūpa śivatattva* is the Light-nature that the aspirant seeks to enter. When one cannot enter the undivided circle (*akhaṇḍamaṇḍala*) directly, one resorts to the *svātantryaśakti* — the power of freedom — and experiences the Bhairava penetration (*bhairavasamāveśa*).

#### What is the difference between Śiva tattva and Śakti tattva?

Śiva tattva is the principle of pure, undifferentiated I-consciousness — the *prakāśa* aspect. It is transcendent (*viśvottīrṇa*), one (*abheda*), and devoid of all duality.

Śakti tattva (the 35th) is the principle of power-in-difference (*bhedabheda*). It is the first stirring of self-differentiation within the unity of Śiva. As Dyczkowski explains (Vol. 1, line ~28926): "The universe exists at these levels in a subtle form, just enough to be experienced as one with Śiva's universal and transcendental consciousness. Thus, this is the sphere of unity-in-difference (bhedabheda), which is that of the Principle of Śakti."

In the Tantrasāra's description of the 36 tattvas (TSā 10.14), the Śiva tattva is the 36th, characterized as *sarvāvacchedaśūnya* — utterly devoid of limitation. Above it, the 37th (Bhairava/Paramaśiva, both immanent and transcendent) and 38th (Anākhya, the Inexplicable) are sometimes added. The Śakti tattva, conversely, is the "primordial vibrating energy that contains within itself the seed of all differentiation."

The relation between them is not dualistic. As stated in Tantrāloka 3.67cd-68ab (Vol. 2, line ~12043): "Their united form (yāmala) is termed dynamic union (saṁghaṭṭa)." The union of Śiva (prakāśa) and Śakti (vimarśa) is the Heart (*hṛdaya*), which is the ultimate reality.

#### How does Śiva tattva function as the substrate of the remaining 35 tattvas?

The Śiva tattva is the *ādya tattva* — the first principle from which the entire hierarchy of 36 principles descends. As Dyczkowski notes (Vol. 1, line ~28938-28952): "All the thirty-six principles form a causal chain such that the immediately preceding group is the cause of the succeeding one. As the higher principles generate the lower, the upper end of the chain is the ultimate uncaused cause of everything."

The Tantrasāra (7th Āhnika) explains the hierarchy:
- From Śiva tattva issues Śakti tattva
- From Śakti tattva issues Sadāśiva tattva (I-This consciousness)
- From Sadāśiva issues Īśvara tattva (This-I consciousness)
- From Īśvara issues Śuddhavidyā (Pure Knowledge)
- Then the descent continues through Māyā and the five *kañcukas* (coverings) of the impure universe, down to Earth

Each lower principle is an *āvaraṇa* (covering) of the higher, and each higher is *vyāpaka* (pervasive) of the lower. The Śiva tattva, being the highest, is the most pervasive — it is the ground and substrate of all. In the *tattvaśuddhi* (purification of principles) ritual, the yogi ascends through all the tattvas to rest in the Śiva tattva, realizing that all lower principles are temporary contractions of this one consciousness.

#### The role of svātantrya (absolute freedom)

*Svātantrya* is the defining characteristic of the Śiva tattva. It is not merely a property but its very nature. As stated in the Tantrasāra (1st Āhnika, line 60): "This Light is independent (svatantra), and by virtue of its freedom (svātantrya), being free of limitation by space, time and form, it is all-pervasive, eternal, and both omniform and formless by nature."

The Tantrasāra further explains (3rd Āhnika, line 69): "When one cannot enter the undivided circle, then, seeing the power of freedom (svātantryaśakti) as preeminent, one experiences the Bhairava penetration free of thought constructs."

Dyczkowski (Vol. 4, line ~8064) notes: "When (the power of creative) freedom (svātantrya) unfolds, it makes the universe one with its own nature." The freedom of the Śiva tattva is not the freedom to choose between options but the unconditional autonomy to manifest the universe without any external constraint or internal necessity.

#### Prakāśa/Vimarśa as the nature of Śiva

The Śiva tattva is constituted by two inseparable aspects:

1. **Prakāśa** (Light): The pure, self-luminous, objectless radiance of consciousness. It is the "that" (*idam*) aspect — sheer manifestness without content.

2. **Vimarśa** (Reflective Awareness): The self-referential subjectivity by which consciousness knows itself as "I." It is the power of self-possession that distinguishes sentient consciousness from insentient matter.

Dyczkowski (Vol. 4, line ~1793) explains: "Perception of an object entails both presentation and representation. The former is called prakāśa and the latter vimarśa." In the ultimate state of Śiva tattva, there is no object — only the pure self-awareness of "I am I" (*aham aham*). The entire universe arises as the *idaṁvimarśa* (reflective awareness of "this") within the *ahaṁvimarśa* (reflective awareness of "I").

Abhinavagupta (on TĀ 3.67cd-68ab, Vol. 2, line ~12055) identifies Anuttara (A) as Prakāśa and Anuttarā (A) as Vimarśa; their dynamic union (*saṁghaṭṭa*) generates Ānanda (Ā), the bliss from which the universe flows.

### Narration Script (Full, ~1500 words)

**[0:00-1:00 — OPENING]**

Of the thirty-six principles that constitute reality in the非dual Śaiva system of Kashmir, the first and highest is the Śiva tattva. It is not a god sitting on a mountain nor a cosmic personage — it is the pure, unconditioned I-consciousness that is the substrate of all existence. Abhinavagupta, the great tenth-century master, opens his Tantrāloka by identifying this principle with the very Light of consciousness itself — *prakāśa* — and its inseparable reflective self-awareness — *vimarśa*. This is not mere theory. The Śiva tattva is your own innermost awareness, recognized as it truly is, free of all contraction.

The Tantrasāra, Abhinava's own summary of the Tantrāloka, begins its third chapter by stating: *yad etat prakāśarūpaṃ śivatattvam uktam* — "this Śiva principle of the nature of Light has been taught." The entire edifice of Tantric metaphysics rests on this single insight: reality at its root is not a thing but a luminous, self-aware subjectivity.

**[1:00-3:00 — THE LIGHT THAT SHINES ALWAYS]**

The foundational verse of this teaching is Tantrāloka 1.52. Let me read it to you:

*jñeyasya hi paraṁ tattvaṁ yaḥ prakāśātmakaḥ śivaḥ*
*na hy aprakāśarūpasya prākāśyaṁ vastutāpi vā*

"The supreme reality of the object of perception is Śiva, who is the Light of consciousness, for that which is not Light can neither be illumined nor even exist."

The argument is devastatingly simple. If something were outside consciousness — "not Light" — it could never appear. But everything we know appears. Therefore, everything that appears is, in its deepest nature, nothing but consciousness itself. Śiva tattva is not a conclusion arrived at by inference; it is the immediate, undeniable presence of experience itself.

Abhinava drives this home in verse 1.54:

*prakāśo nāma yaś cāyaṁ sarvadaiva prakāśate*
*anapahnavanīyatvāt kiṁ tasmin mānakalpanaiḥ*

"Indeed, this Light shines at all times; so, as it cannot be denied, what use are contrived means of knowledge to prove it?"

The light of consciousness is self-proving — *ādisiddha*, proved right from the start. Every attempt to prove it already presupposes it. Every attempt to deny it is itself a mode of its shining. Even the Buddhist who denies the self, says Abhinava in verse 1.56, experiences his own denial as "it manifests to me." The negator is himself the Light he seeks to negate.

Jayaratha, commenting on this passage, cites the *Mālinīvijayavārttika*: "The radiant flashing forth of the wonderful variety of all things is like the sprouts and flowers of the tree of the reflective awareness of one's own Self."

**[3:00-5:00 — PRAKĀŚA AND VIMARŚA]**

Consciousness is not a blank light. It is light that knows itself as light. This self-knowledge is vimarśa. Dyczkowski explains it precisely: perception involves both presentation — prakāśa, the sheer fact of something appearing — and representation — vimarśa, the cognitive response that ascertains what appears.

In the ultimate state of Śiva tattva, there is no object. The reflective awareness turns inward as pure self-possession: *ahaṁvimarśa* — "I am I." This is not the ego, which is a contraction. It is the infinite I-consciousness that the *Spandaśāstra* calls "the repose of the light within its own nature."

The Tantrāloka's third chapter describes how the union of Śiva as Prakāśa and Śakti as Vimarśa generates the dynamic pulsation of bliss from which the universe unfolds. The letter A — Anuttara — represents the Light. The second A — Anuttarā — represents its reflective awareness. Their union, *saṁghaṭṭa*, produces the long Ā — Ānanda, bliss. From this bliss arises the will to be all things, then the knowledge of them, then the action that actualizes them.

Thus the entire universe is nothing but the play of Śiva's self-awareness — the exuberance of I-consciousity reflecting upon itself in infinite self-delight.

**[5:00-7:00 — SVĀTANTRYA: ABSOLUTE FREEDOM]**

The defining characteristic of the Śiva tattva is *svātantrya* — absolute freedom. This is not the freedom to choose between alternatives. It is the unconditioned autonomy of consciousness to manifest the universe without any external constraint or internal necessity.

The Tantrasāra states: *eka eva prakāśaḥ... svatantra ekaḥ prakāśaḥ svātantryād eva ca deśakālākārāvacchedavirahāt vyāpako nityaḥ sarvākāranirākārasvabhāvaḥ* — "This Light is one... independent. By virtue of its freedom, being free of limitation by space, time and form, it is all-pervasive, eternal, and both omniform and formless by nature."

When this freedom unfolds, it makes the universe one with its own nature. The limiting conditions of space, time, and form do not bind Śiva; rather, they are appearances *within* Śiva. As Abhinava declares in 1.59cd-60ab:

*anapekṣasya vaśino deśakālākṛtikramāḥ*
*niyatā neti sa vibhur nityo viśvākṛtiḥ śivaḥ*

"For one who is independent and the master, spatial, temporal and formal processes are not restricted, and so He is Śiva, who is ubiquitous, eternal, and omniform."

Jayaratha explains: space cannot divide Him (He is omnipresent), time cannot sequence Him (He is eternal), form cannot contain Him (He is all forms).

**[7:00-8:30 — THE HIERARCHY OF TATTVAS]**

The Śiva tattva is the first of thirty-six principles arranged in a descending hierarchy. From Śiva issues Śakti tattva — the principle of power-in-difference. From Śakti issues Sadāśiva — "I am This" consciousness. From Sadāśiva issues Īśvara — "This is I" consciousness. From Īśvara issues Śuddhavidyā — pure knowledge.

The descent continues through Māyā and its five coverings — the *kañcukas* of limited agency, knowledge, attachment, time and causality — down through the individual soul, nature, intellect, ego, mind, senses, and finally the gross elements, ending with Earth.

Each successive principle is an *āvaraṇa* — a covering — of the one above it. Each is less pervasive than the one above. Śiva tattva, being the highest, is the *most* pervasive — it is the unlimited space of consciousness in which all other principles appear as reflections.

As the Tantrasāra tenth chapter states: *sarvāvacchedaśūnyaṃ śivatattvaṃ ṣaṭtriṃśam* — "the Śiva tattva, the thirty-sixth, is utterly devoid of all limitation." Above it, in the reckoning of the non-dual Śaivas, are the thirty-seventh and thirty-eighth principles — Paramaśiva/Bhairava and the Inexplicable Anākhya — but for most purposes, the Śiva tattva functions as the ultimate ground.

**[8:30-9:30 — ANUTTARA AND THE HEART]**

The supreme designation for this reality in Abhinava's system is Anuttara — the Absolute beyond which there is nothing. Anuttara is both the source and the nature of the Śiva tattva. It is the *hṛdaya* — the Heart — which is the union of Śiva and Śakti, Prakāśa and Vimarśa.

This Heart is not a physical organ but the core of one's own being, experienced in the clearest moments of self-awareness. As Abhinava writes in his *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa*: "Hṛt is consciousness, which is the location of the ultimate foundation of all things. Its 'ayas' are the various cognitions that are the relative distinctions conceived by its own inherent freedom."

To know the Śiva tattva is not to acquire new information but to recognize what one has always already been. This is *pratyabhijñā* — re-cognition. The Light does not need to be lit; it only needs to be seen as what it is.

**[9:30-10:00 — CLOSING]**

The Śiva tattva is thus the first principle and the final truth. It is the translucent mirror of I-consciousness in which the entire universe appears as a reflection. It is Prakāśa — the Light that illumines all things without itself being illumined by another. It is Vimarśa — the self-possession that knows itself as "I" in the wonder of its own freedom.

As Abhinava declares in verse 1.55: the supreme living essence of all the means of knowledge, of all objects, of all perceivers — that same Supreme Lord is our own innermost Self, the Śiva tattva, the Heart of all.

To recognize this is liberation. To live in this recognition is the goal of all Trika practice. To name it — Śiva tattva — is already to point to the unnameable radiance that is one's own being.

### Connections to Other Doctrines

#### How this relates to Bhairava

Bhairava is the thirty-seventh principle — the *paramaśiva* who is both transcendent and immanent, both the pure Light of Śiva tattva and the power to manifest the universe. If Śiva tattva is pure oneness (*abheda*), Bhairava is the *bhedābheda* — the unity-in-difference that encompasses both transcendence and immanence. The Tantrasāra (8th Āhnika) identifies the *mahāprakāśarūpa* (Great Light-nature) as *paramaśivarūpa* — the form of Supreme Śiva. Bhairava is thus the dynamic fullness of the Śiva tattva, the Lord who both withdraws and emits the universe by his freedom.

Dyczkowski explains (Vol. 1, note 18): "The thirty-six principles, up to Śiva without Support, are such as they are according to the degree in which they penetrate into consciousness, which is the Supreme Bhairava." The Śiva tattva is Bhairava's nature as pure consciousness; Bhairava is the same reality experienced as the fullness that contains and transcends the tattvas.

#### How this relates to the Trika Triad

The Trika system is built around three principles: Śiva (the subject), Śakti (the power/means), and Nara (the object/individual soul). The Śiva tattva corresponds to the first — pure subjectivity. The Trika goddesses — Parā, Parāparā, and Aparā — correspond to these three. Parā is the supreme goddess of I-consciousness identified with the Śiva tattva. Parāparā is the middling goddess of the Śakti tattva (subject-object relation). Aparā is the lower goddess of the objective universe (Nara).

As Dyczkowski notes (Vol. 1, line ~3249): "In Abhinava's exegeses of the Trika triad, the first three are the three Goddesses. Parā is the subject; Parāparā is the means of knowledge and Aparā is the object." The Śiva tattva as Parā is thus the foundation of the Trika — the subject in whom the means and objects of knowledge dissolve.

#### How this relates to Spanda

Spanda — the "vibration" or subtle dynamism of consciousness — is the activity of the Śiva tattva. It is not a movement in space or time but the intrinsic pulsation of I-consciousness, the eternal self-affirmation of "I-I" (*aham aham*). The Tantrāloka (1/1 comm.) identifies Spanda with the Heart and describes it as *sāmānyaspanda* — the universal vibration.

The relation is: Śiva tattva is the *substance* (the Light itself), while Spanda is the *activity* (the dynamism of that Light). They are not two things. The Spandaśāstra teaches that the repose of the Light within its own nature *is* the state of pure I-consciousness. Prayer: the *sakṛd vibhāta* dictum — "this Self shines forth but once" — describes the timeless self-manifestation of the Śiva tattva, which is the Spanda of consciousness.
