# Deep-Dive Video Essay: Naimittika and Nitya — The Two Kinds of Rites

**Source:** Tantrāloka Vol. 10, Chapter 28, Verses 2-9 (~lines 1186-1429)
**Duration:** 10 minutes

## Opening Hook (0:00-0:45)
Abhinavagupta opens Chapter 28 by questioning the very distinction between "regular" (nitya) and "occasional" (naimittika) rites. If a regular rite is defined as one that must always be performed, no rite qualifies — not even daily sandhyāvandana, since nobody worships 24/7. This philosophical deconstruction sets the stage for a radical redefinition.

## Part 1: The Logic of Nitya vs. Naimittika (0:45-3:00)
- Verse 2 (~line 1186): "niyataṁ bhāvi yan nityaṁ tad ity asmin vidhau sthite | mukhyatvaṁ tanmayībhūtaṁ sarvaṁ naimittikaṁ tataḥ" — If regular means fixed, then all rites are occasional since none are performed constantly
- Verse 3 (~line 1217): Even daily rites are fixed by conceived divisions of day, month, asterism, year — so theoretically all could be called "regular"
- Verse 4 (~line 1257): Abhinava's own definition — a naimittika rite is: (1) not subject to doubt, (2) necessary, (3) occurs occasionally, (4) not obligatory for all
- Verse 5 (~line 1304): Sandhyā, parvan worship, and pavitraka are nitya for all followers of the Trika scripture

## Part 2: The Twenty-Three Occasions (3:00-5:30)
- Verses 6-9 (~line 1363): Complete catalog of 23 kinds of occasional events from the Tantrasāra
- **Key occasions listed:** Acquisition of knowledge/scriptures, teacher's visit/ family, teacher's birthday/consecration/death day, one's own birthday, festivals, śrāddha, calamities, rejoicing, portents, Yoginī meeting (melaka), reunion with spiritual lineage, beginning/middle/end of scripture explanation, kramodaya, deity visions in dreams, ājñā (teacher authorization), expiation for rule infringements
- Jayaratha's commentary: explains each term — "calamity" includes theft of one's Śakti, "rejoicing" is recovery of what was taken
- Note on Śivarātrī: the four things that happened to the Lord that night are common to all scriptures, so Abhinava doesn't elaborate

## Part 3: Kinds of Auspicious Days — Yogasaṁcāra (5:30-7:30)
- Verse 10-14ab (~line 1457): Two kinds of parvan — Kula and Akula
- Eight Kula Mothers and their lunar days:
  - Māheśī: 14th and 7th
  - Brāhmī: 1st and 15th
  - Kaumārī: 6th and 3rd
  - Vaiṣṇavī: 4th and 12th
  - Aindrī: 5th and 13th
  - Vārāhī: 9th and 11th
  - Caṇḍī (Cāmuṇḍā): 8th and 2nd
  - Yogeśī: 10th (and shares 8th with Cāmuṇḍā)
- The lunar day one is born on is one's Kula day — "whatever lunar day one is born on, that is his family deity (kuladevatā)"
- Akula days are the same but in the dark fortnight, metaphorically "reversed"

## Part 4: Philosophical Core — Time as Consciousness (7:30-9:30)
- Abhinava derives parvan from √pṝ "to fill" — a parvan is a time when consciousness is "filled"
- The movement of the vital breath through cycles generates consciousness; a parvan is when the plenitude of one's own consciousness is attained (verse 15-18ab, ~line 1689)
- Inner equivalent of parvans is accessible only to those practiced in observing the breath cycles (Chapter 6)
- The parvan is "the cow that grants all desires" because consciousness is then full and unconditioned

## Conclusion (9:30-10:00)
- Bridge to next topic: The 23 occasions include the collective assembly (melaka) — which leads to Abhinava's remarkable analysis of collective consciousness in verses 18cd-25ab
- Key insight: For Abhinava, ritual is never mere external action — it is always a technology of consciousness
