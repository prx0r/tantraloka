# THE FOUR KĀLĪS OF SUBJECTIVITY — PARAMĀRKA, KĀLĀGNIRUDRA, MAHĀKĀLA, MAHĀBHAIRAVĪ
## 10-Minute Deep-Dive Video Essay — Tantrāloka Vol. 3, Chapter 4, Verses 164–173ab

---

### Core Teaching

The third and final group of four Kālīs governs the sphere of subjectivity (*pramātṛ*). Having consumed objectivity into the means of knowledge, and the means of knowledge into their source, consciousness now turns upon itself — the subject itself becomes the object of scrutiny. The pattern repeats: Paramārkakālī emanates the subject; Kālāgnirudrakālī sustains it; Mahākālakālī withdraws it; and Mahābhairavaghoracaṇḍakālī — the thirteenth and central Kālī — represents the inexplicable state of the subject, which is the consummation of the entire cycle. This final Kālī is also the source and ground of all twelve, the one who is in the "middle" of the wheel.

**Line references:** TĀ 4/164–173ab; Dyczkowski translation pp. 236–252, lines 17600–18120.

---

### Paramārkakālī (Verse 164) — The Supreme Sun; Emanation of the Subject

Paramārka means "the Supreme Sun." Whereas Mārtaṇḍakālī was the sun of the means of knowledge, Paramārka is the sun of the subject — the light by which the knower knows itself.

Jayaratha explains (lines 14487–14490): As Paramārkakālī, "in the phase of the emission of the subject (*pramātṛsṛṣṭi*), it also generates the lord of the senses, (into which the ego that controls the twelve senses dissolves away)."

The transition: after the means of knowledge have been fully consumed into the inexplicable (Mārtaṇḍa), the subject — which had been implicit throughout — now becomes explicit. The "lord of the senses" emerges as the inner controller of the cognitive apparatus. This is the first dawn of subjectivity as a distinct phase of consciousness.

---

### Kālāgnirudrakālī (Verses 165–168) — The Fire of Time; Persistence of the Subject

Kālāgnirudra is Rudra as the Fire of Time — the destructive aspect of time that both consumes and sustains. This Kālī governs the persistence of the subject phase.

Jayaratha explains (lines 14490–14494): "In the phase of the persistence of the subject (*pramātṛsthiti*), it also generates (the higher) nature of the conceived perceiver within (the domain of) Māyā (that came to the fore in the previous phase, which now merges into the transcendental subject, which is its higher nature)."

As the Fire of Time, this Kālī has a dual aspect: she burns away the limited ego while simultaneously sustaining the expanded sense of self that emerges from that burning. The subject persists not as a fixed entity but as a dynamic process of self-awareness.

---

### Mahākālakālī (Verses 169–170) — The Great Time; Withdrawal of the Subject

Mahākāla is "the Great Time" — time beyond time, the dimension of Śiva's eternal presence. Mahākālakālī governs the withdrawal of the subject phase.

Jayaratha explains (lines 14495–14499): "In the phase of the withdrawal of the subject (*pramātṛsaṁhāra*), it generates the perceiver, who delights in grasping the expansion (of consciousness), intent on abandoning contraction."

Gnoli's interpretation (lines 17944–17946): According to Gnoli, only verse 170 describes Mahābhairavaghoracaṇḍakālī, and he reads verse 169 along with 168 as a description of Mahākālakālī. Jayaratha takes verses 169–170 as describing Mahākālakālī.

---

### Mahābhairavaghoracaṇḍakālī (Verses 171–173ab) — The Thirteenth; The Consummation

This is the longest name among the Kālīs: Mahābhairavaghoracaṇḍakālī — "The Horrific and Wrathful Kālī of the Great Bhairava." She is the thirteenth Kālī, occupying the center of the wheel, from whom the other twelve emanate and into whom they dissolve.

Jayaratha explains (lines 14499–14503): "In the phase of the inexplicable state of the subject (*pramātṛanākhya*), it also generates the (fully) expanded form (of pure dynamic consciousness, which contains all the phases as its innate creative power)."

**The Central Position (lines 14910–14912):** "Mahābhairavacaṇḍograghorakālī is in the middle of the other twelve, who originate from her." She is both the beginning and the end — the one Goddess who precedes emanation and remains after dissolution.

---

### The Three Inexplicable States

Each group of four Kālīs ends with an inexplicable (*anākhya*) state:

| Sphere | Inexplicable Kālī | Nature of the Inexplicable |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------------|
| Objectivity | Yamakālī | Object neither exists nor does not exist |
| Means of Knowledge | Mārtaṇḍakālī | The senses dissolve into the sun of consciousness |
| Subjectivity | Mahābhairavaghoracaṇḍakālī | Subject transcends all categories |

Mahābhairavī is the *anākhya* of the subject — the state in which even the most subtle sense of "I" dissolves into the pure consciousness of Bhairava, the Supreme Śiva. This is not annihilation but the fullest expansion (*vikāsa*) of consciousness.

---

### The Thirteen Kālīs System

Jayaratha notes (lines 15435–15443) that the Kramastotra and related texts present *thirteen* Kālīs — the twelve plus the central one, Mahābhairavaghoracaṇḍakālī. The sequence according to the *Cidgaganacandrikā*:

1–3. Sṛṣṭī, Sthitināśa, Saṁhāra
4. Raktakālī
5. Sukālī
6. Yamakālī
7. Mṛtyukālī
8. Bhadrakālī
9. Paramārkakālī
10. Mārtaṇḍakālī
11. Kālāgnirudrakālī
12. Mahākālakālī
13. Mahābhairavaghoracaṇḍakālī

---

### The Complete Devouring

The entire process of the Twelve Kālīs is one of progressive consumption (*grāsa*) culminating in the "Great Devouring" (*mahāgrāsa*) by Mahābhairavī. Swami Lakshmanjoo explains (lines 14551–14620): when the yogi attains this state, the distinction between contemplation and emergence from contemplation disappears. This is *nirvyuthānasamādhi* — "contemplation without emergence" — called "Cosmic Bliss" (*jagadānanda*), which "is considered to be much more excellent than all the other planes of bliss."

---

### Philosophical Significance

1. **Subjectivity as the Final Frontier**: The third group reveals that even the most subtle sense of self is a phase of consciousness that arises, persists, and dissolves. The "death" of the ego is not the end but the gateway to the supreme expansion.

2. **The Thirteenth as the One and the Many**: Mahābhairavaghoracaṇḍakālī is both the thirteenth (counted separately) and the one from whom the twelve arise. The wheel of time has a center that is beyond time — the still point of the turning world.

3. **Horror and Auspiciousness**: The names of the final Kālīs emphasize terror — "Fire of Time," "Great Death," "Horrific and Wrathful" — yet this terror is the face of grace. The dissolution of the subject is frightening to the ego but blissful to consciousness itself.

4. **The Consummation of Śāktopāya**: The journey through the Twelve Kālīs completes the work of Śāktopāya. By tracing consciousness through objectivity, means, and subjectivity, the practitioner arrives at the pure subjectivity of Bhairava — the goal of the Empowered Means.
