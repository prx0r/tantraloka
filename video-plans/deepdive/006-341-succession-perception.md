# 341 — Succession and Perception
**Tantrāloka 9.17cd–20ab** | Chapter 9: Kāryakāraṇa (Causality)

## Hook
Do we perceive succession because time is real, or is time a form of perception?

## Core Teaching
Succession is not a feature of objects themselves but of the perceiving subject. The universal perceiver — Śiva — manifests objects in succession as part of His free creative play.

## Key Argument
The passage argues that:
1. Objects do not intrinsically possess "before" and "after"
2. Priority and subsequence are relational — require a perceiver who holds both in awareness
3. Without a conscious subject connecting past and present, there is no succession
4. Therefore, succession is the nature of the perceiver, not the perceived

## The Unifying Subject
For A to be "before" B, there must be something that knows both A and B. This something is:
- Not A (A doesn't know B)
- Not B (B doesn't remember A)
- Not a third object (objects don't relate themselves)
- **Consciousness** — which holds past, present, and future in a single non-successive awareness

## The Pratyabhijñā Principle
This argument draws on Utpaladeva's insight: recognition (*pratyabhijñā*) — "I am the same one who saw this before" — proves the continuity of consciousness across time. If consciousness were momentary, recognition would be impossible.

## Connection to Non-Successive Consciousness
Abhinavagupta's ultimate point: consciousness is *akrama* (non-successive). Succession appears because consciousness projects forms in sequence, but it itself never moves. This is the foundation for the causality-as-manifestation doctrine.

## Philosophical Stakes
- Refutes realism: time is not an independent category
- Refutes idealism of the Buddhist sort: consciousness is substantial and continuous
- Establishes phenomenology: the structure of experience reveals the nature of reality
