# STHITINĀŚAKĀLĪ AND YAMAKĀLĪ — THE DESTRUCTION OF PERSISTENCE AND THE INEXPLICABLE STATE OF OBJECTIVITY
## 10-Minute Deep-Dive Video Essay — Tantrāloka Vol. 3, Chapter 4, Verses 150bcd–151

---

### Core Teaching

The third and fourth Kālīs complete the first group governing objectivity (*prameya*). Sthitināśakālī ("the Kālī of the Destruction of Persistence") withdraws the stable objectivity established by Raktakālī, initiating the retraction of the object back into consciousness. Yamakālī ("the Kālī of Restriction") governs the inexplicable state (*anākhya*) of objectivity — the phase in which the object is neither fully present nor absent, caught between existence and non-existence. The word *yama* means "restriction" or "control" — this Kālī restricts the flow of objectivity, introducing doubt that serves as the pivot to the next group of Kālīs (the means of knowledge). This four-phase structure within the sphere of objectivity recapitulates the universal pattern of emanation, persistence, withdrawal, and inexplicability.

**Line references:** TĀ 4/150bcd–151; Dyczkowski translation pp. 209–218, lines 15350–16220.

---

### Sthitināśakālī (Verse 150bcd) — The Destruction of Persistence

**Sanskrit (partial):**
```
sthitināśakālī || 150bcd ||
```

Jayaratha explains (lines 14454–14462): "As Raktakālī, assuming a state of attachment towards objectivity in the phase of its retraction into consciousness, there within itself consciousness generates that same state, but this time with the intention of retracting it back inwardly into itself."

Sthitināśakālī is the turning point: the outward flow of sensory engagement (Raktakālī) reverses direction. The object, which had been stabilized through attachment, now begins to dissolve back into its source. This is not yet withdrawal — it is the *destruction of the stability* that makes withdrawal possible.

---

### The Transition

Swami Lakshmanjoo explains (lines 15090–15115): The moment when consciousness, having been fully engaged with the object through the senses, begins to release its attachment. The "coloring" of the object fades, and the object's apparent independence diminishes. The yogi experiences this as the natural dissolution of the perceptual field — objects lose their solidity and begin to merge back into the light of consciousness.

---

### Yamakālī (Verse 151) — The Inexplicable State of Objectivity

**Sanskrit (line 14462):**
```
yamaś ca śaṅkā niyamanaṁ tayā saha || 151 ||
```
**Dyczkowski Translation (lines 14462–14464):** "Then (as Yamakālī, who operates in the Inexplicable state of the object), consciousness generates and consumes the (sense of) doubt which (instigates thought, and so) is an obstacle to the retraction of that objectivity back into itself."

The word *yama* means "restriction" or "control." Yamakālī restricts the flow of objective cognition, introducing a phase of suspension. In this state:

1. The object is no longer clearly present (as in persistence)
2. The object is not yet fully absent (as in withdrawal)
3. A sense of doubt (*śaṅkā*) arises — "Is this object real? Did I perceive it? What is its nature?"

This doubt is not mere uncertainty but a positive cognitive state — the recognition that the object's independent existence is questionable. It is the "inexplicable" (*anākhya*) state because the object cannot be categorized as either existent or non-existent.

---

### The Fourfold Pattern in the First Group

The first four Kālīs demonstrate the complete cycle within objectivity:

| Kālī | Phase | Cognitive State |
|------|-------|-----------------|
| 1. Sṛṣṭikālī | Emanation | Object first appears in consciousness |
| 2. Raktakālī | Persistence | Object stabilized through sensory attachment |
| 3. Sthitināśakālī | Withdrawal | Object's stability dissolves |
| 4. Yamakālī | Inexplicable | Object in indefinable state; doubt arises |

This is the first iteration of the four-phase pattern that will repeat for the means of knowledge and for subjectivity.

---

### The Function of Doubt

Jayaratha explains (lines 16220–16298): The doubt generated by Yamakālī serves as the pivot to the next cognitive sphere. When the object can no longer be sustained or clearly defined, consciousness naturally turns to the means by which it knew the object. The doubt "What did I perceive?" transforms into "How did I perceive it?" — shifting attention from the object to the cognitive apparatus.

This is the "great devouring and the great sport" (*mahāgrāsa-mahāvilāsa*) mentioned in the Kramastotra (lines 16200–16218): the goddess "assumes an introverted state and merges all the universe into that state" while simultaneously "projecting all phenomena into an outer form and enjoying the aesthetic savor of the Great Sport."

---

### The Kālī of Restriction as Liberation

The restriction (*yama*) imposed by Yamakālī is paradoxical: it is both an obstacle and a liberation. It is an obstacle because doubt prevents the clean retraction of objectivity. It is liberation because doubt reveals the constructed nature of objectivity, preparing consciousness for the next stage of interiorization.

Swami Lakshmanjoo explains (lines 16259–16284): "When the yogi is in this state of the Inexplicable, he experiences both forms — concealment and unfolding of its own nature. Then, by his teacher's grace, he begins to withdraw, root and all, the extroverted modality which is the concealment of his own true nature."

---

### Scriptural Quotations

**Kramastotra (quoted in Cidgaganacandrikā):**
"I worship Yamakālī, who is the Great Devouring and the Great Sport, dissolving embodiment into Her, entering into the essential nature of Parā."

---

### Philosophical Significance

1. **The Inexplicable as a Positive State**: The *anākhya* is not mere absence but a positive cognitive state — the recognition that reality transcends the categories of existence and non-existence.

2. **Doubt as a Spiritual Catalyst**: Far from being an impediment, doubt (when properly understood) drives consciousness deeper. It is the recognition that the conceptual frameworks we use to grasp reality are inadequate.

3. **The Rhythm of Consciousness**: The four-phase pattern — emanation, persistence, withdrawal, inexplicable — is not arbitrary but reflects the fundamental rhythm of consciousness. Every perception follows this arc.

4. **The Completion of Objectivity**: With Yamakālī, the sphere of objectivity is complete. Consciousness has experienced object-formation, object-stabilization, object-dissolution, and the indefinable state beyond all three. It is now ready to turn to the means by which objects are known.
