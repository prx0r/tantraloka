# THE ARISING OF THE CYCLES OF CONSCIOUSNESS (SAṀVIDCAKRODAYA)
## 10-Minute Deep-Dive Video Essay — Tantrāloka Vol. 3, Chapter 4, Verses 127–146ab

---

### Core Teaching

Before presenting the Twelve Kālīs, Abhinavagupta introduces a Yoga from the *Yogasaṁcāra* — a teaching on the arising of the cycles of consciousness (*saṁvidcakrodaya*). This section bridges the earlier discussion of inner worship and the rejection of external ritual with the full exposition of the Kālīs. The central teaching is that consciousness manifests in cycles or wheels (*cakra*) — configurations of cognitive energies that can be directly experienced by the yogi. These wheels — of sixteen, twelve, eight, and four spokes — correspond to the four fundamental structures of cognitive experience: objective, subjective, and the two modes of their interaction. The teaching serves as a yogic preparation for the full revelation of the Twelve Kālīs.

**Line references:** TĀ 4/127–146ab; Dyczkowski translation pp. 167–195, lines 11144–14449.

---

### The Four Wheels of Consciousness

Jayaratha explains (lines 11350–11397) the basic structure of the Krama system as a series of four wheels (*cakra*):

| Wheel | Spokes | Symbol | Cognitive Domain |
|-------|--------|--------|-----------------|
| 1st | 16 | Moon | Emanation (*sṛṣṭi*) |
| 2nd | 12 | Sun | Persistence (*sthiti*) |
| 3rd | 8 | Fire | Withdrawal (*saṁhāra*) |
| 4th | 4 | Light | Inexplicable (*anākhya*) |

The twelve-spoked wheel (the Sun) is the one that Abhinavagupta develops as the Twelve Kālīs. The sixteen-spoked wheel (the Moon) and eight-spoked wheel (Fire) are intermediate configurations. The four-spoked wheel (Light) is the ground of all three.

---

### The Sixteen-Spoked Wheel (Verses 127–130)

The first wheel has sixteen spokes, corresponding to the sixteen digits (*kalā*) of the Moon. Jayaratha explains: "Although the Wheel here is the Inexplicable, the object of knowledge predominates, and so as it is the Moon of objectivity, it is white" (lines 12437–12440).

The sixteen goddesses of this wheel include:
- Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī (the Attractress of Time)
- Mātṛsadbhāva (the Essence of Subjects)
- Vāmeśvarī (the Sovereign Goddess of the Left)
- And thirteen other goddesses completing the set

These sixteen represent the fullness of objectivity before it condenses into the twelve-fold structure of the Sun Wheel.

---

### The Twelve-Spoked Wheel — Transition

"When a yogi is well established in this teaching (of the sixteen-spoked wheel), the wheel of twelve (spokes) is said to arise" (lines 12470–12500). The twelve-spoked wheel is the Sun, whose rays are the twelve Kālīs. This wheel governs the sphere of the means of knowledge and marks the transition from predominantly objective consciousness to the recognition of the cognitive apparatus.

---

### The Eight-Spoked Wheel (Verses 131–140)

The third wheel has eight spokes, corresponding to the eight forms of Fire. This wheel governs the withdrawal of both objectivity and the means of knowledge, preparing the ground for the pure subjectivity of the fourth wheel. The eight spokes are the eight energies that consume the twelve-fold structure.

---

### The Four-Spoked Wheel (Verses 141–146ab)

The fourth wheel has four spokes — the Light of consciousness itself. Jayaratha explains: "The fourth Wheel, which is like the fifth, is the Light of consciousness itself, contains four digits, one for each of the three Wheels, and one of its own" (lines 11392–11394).

The four spokes correspond to:
1. The emanation wheel
2. The persistence wheel
3. The withdrawal wheel
4. The inexplicable (the encompassing fourth)

This wheel is both one of the four and the ground of the other three. It is the "fifth" in the sense that it transcends and includes the four.

---

### The Yogic Practice

The *Yogasaṁcāra* teaches a specific contemplative method (lines 12523–12700): the yogi visualizes these wheels within consciousness, tracing the flow of cognitive energies through their configurations. This is not a mere mental exercise but an actual restructuring of perception — the yogi learns to experience every cognitive act as the rotation of these wheels.

Jayaratha notes (lines 12466–12469) that he "did not have access to the Yogasaṁcāra" and that the text quoted is "drawn from" it. The teaching is thus fragmentary but sufficient to convey the essential method.

---

### Connection to the Twelve Kālīs

This section (4/127–146ab) prepares the ground for the full exposition of the Twelve Kālīs (4/146cd–173ab). The yogi who has practiced the four wheels is ready to understand the specific operations of the twelve goddesses. The twelve Kālīs are the spokes of the Sun wheel — the central wheel that governs the entire cognitive process.

Jayaratha states (lines 11350–11352): "Abhinava is now going to expound his teaching concerning the 'inner' sequence of the phases of consciousness (*saṁvitkrama*), that correspond to the sequence in which the assembly of Kālīs are worshipped externally."

---

### Philosophical Significance

1. **Consciousness as Geometry**: The wheels of consciousness are not metaphorical — they are the actual structure of cognitive experience. The yogi learns to perceive the geometry of awareness itself.

2. **From Many to One**: The progression from sixteen to twelve to eight to four is a progressive simplification. Each wheel contains the previous ones but at a higher level of integration.

3. **The Four as the Matrix**: The four-spoked wheel is the most fundamental. Its structure — emanation, persistence, withdrawal, inexplicable — recurs at every level of the system, from the macrocosmic to the microcosmic.

4. **Practice Precedes Doctrine**: Abhinavagupta does not simply present the Twelve Kālīs as doctrine. He first gives a yogic practice (from the Yogasaṁcāra) that prepares the practitioner to directly experience the structure he will then explain.
