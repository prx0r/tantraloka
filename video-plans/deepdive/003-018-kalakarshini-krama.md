# KĀLAKARṢIṆĪ AND THE KRAMA LINEAGES — THE DESTROYER OF TIME
## 10-Minute Deep-Dive Video Essay — Tantrāloka Vol. 3, Chapter 4, Verses 173cd–181ab

---

### Core Teaching

The Krama system (*Kramanaya*), also called the Great Reality (*Mahārtha*), is the school of Kaula Śaivism centered on the goddess Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī — "She Who Attracts/Destroys Time." Abhinavagupta presents the Krama as the esoteric heart of the Twelve Kālīs: the entire cycle is the play of Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī, who "measures out" (*kalayati*) the five operations (*kalana*) of cosmic cognitive activity through her dynamic pulsation. The Krama lineages are traced through a succession of realized teachers, and the dynamism of the goddess is expressed through three fundamental powers: Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī herself, Mātṛsadbhāva (the Essence of Subjects), and Vāmeśvarī (the Sovereign Goddess of the Left).

**Line references:** TĀ 4/173cd–181ab; Dyczkowski translation pp. 253–279, lines 20011–20660.

### Key Verses

#### The Five Operations of Kālakarṣiṇī (Verses 173cd–175)

**Sanskrit (line 20215):**
```
kalanā kālī kālakarṣiṇī ceti || 176 ||
```
**Dyczkowski Translation:** "(This) measuring/operation (*kalanā*) is called Kālī and Kālakarṣiṇī (the Destroyer of Time)."

Jayaratha explains (lines 20011–20100): The verb *kal* (from which *Kālī* and *kalanā* derive) has five meanings, each corresponding to an operation of the goddess:

1. **Kṣepa** (casting forth) — emission of phenomena
2. **Jñāna** (knowledge) — cognition of phenomena
3. **Saṁkhyāna** (enumeration) — discrimination and ordering
4. **Gati** (movement) — the dynamic flow of consciousness
5. **Nāda** (sound) — the subtle vibration that underlies all manifestation

These five operations correspond to the five acts (*pañcakṛtya*) of Śiva: creation, persistence, destruction, concealment, and grace. Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī is the power that performs all five simultaneously, "measuring out" the universe of time and consciousness.

---

#### Verse 176 — Kālī as the Destroyer of Time

**Sanskrit:**
```
kalanā kālī kālakarṣiṇī ceti |
```
Jayaratha explains (lines 20215–20260): "The process of 'measuring out' (*kalanā*) is the goddess Kālī, who is Kālakarṣiṇī — the Attractress of Time." She is called Kālī because she devours time (*kāla*), and Kālakarṣiṇī because she draws time into herself, consuming the entire cycle of temporal existence into the eternal present of consciousness.

This is the central teaching of the Krama: time is not a container within which events occur but a cognitive construction that consciousness both generates and consumes. Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī is the supreme subject who "eats" time itself.

---

#### The Dynamism of Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī (Verses 176–181ab)

Jayaratha elaborates (lines 20206–20660) on the three goddesses that constitute the dynamism of the Krama:

1. **Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī** — The Attractress of Time, the supreme goddess who manifests as the cognitive cycle
2. **Mātṛsadbhāva** — "The Essence of (all) Subjects," the ground of subjectivity in which all perceivers are united
3. **Vāmeśvarī** — "The Sovereign Goddess of the Left," the power of emanation that flows from the unity of the first two

These three correspond to the three fundamental powers of consciousness: *prakāśa* (light), *vimarśa* (reflective awareness), and their dynamic unity.

### The Krama Lineages

Jayaratha traces the transmission of the Krama teachings (lines 11350–11480; 12420–12500). The Krama emerges from the *Jayadrathayāmala* (JY), the "Venerable King of the Tantras," a vast work in four *ṣaṭkas* of 6,000 verses each. The first *ṣaṭka* is "originally a self-contained work on the cult of Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī" (lines 11363–11364).

The Krama transmission includes:

- The *Kramasadbhāva* — a foundational text on the Krama
- The *Kālikulapañcaśataka* (also called *Devīpañcaśatika*) — the source for the Twelve Kālīs
- The *Kramastotra* by Jñānanetra — a hymn to the thirteen Kālīs
- *Kramastotra* by Abhinavagupta himself (Appendix F, lines 512–514)
- The *Mahānayaprakāśa* by Arṇasimha (Appendix H, lines 521–522)

### The Four Wheels of the Krama

The Krama system organizes the goddesses into four Wheels (*cakra*), as explained at lines 11350–11397:

| Wheel | Number of Kālīs | Symbol | Element |
|-------|----------------|--------|---------|
| Emanation (*sṛṣṭi*) | 16 | Moon | White |
| Persistence (*sthiti*) | 12 | Sun | Red |
| Withdrawal (*saṁhāra*) | 8 | Fire | Black |
| Inexplicable (*anākhya*) | 4 | Light of consciousness | Pure |

Abhinavagupta is "not concerned with their outer worship" and "refers only to the twelve Kālīs of the Wheel of the Sun by name" (lines 11396–11397). The four Wheels become four phases within the single cycle of twelve.

### The Krama and the Trika

Jayaratha states (line 14701) that the Anuttara Trika is "closely related to the Krama system (*kramanayasodara*)." The Krama provides the dynamic, goddess-centered framework that complements the more Śiva-focused Trika. Together, they present the full spectrum of nondual Śaiva theology:

- **Trika**: Focus on the triad (Śiva, Śakti, Nara) and the three means
- **Krama**: Focus on the twelve Kālīs, Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī, and the cognitive cycle

Abhinavagupta's genius is to synthesize both into a single, comprehensive path.

---

### Philosophical Significance

1. **Time as a Cognitive Construct**: Time is not an objective container but a structure of consciousness. Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī "destroys" time by revealing its cognitive nature — time is the "measuring out" (*kalanā*) of consciousness, not a dimension independent of it.

2. **The Five Operations as the Grammar of Experience**: The five meanings of *kal* provide a complete grammar of cognitive experience — emission (intention), knowledge (cognition), enumeration (discrimination), movement (flow), and sound (subtle vibration).

3. **Krama as Practice**: The "sequence" (*krama*) is not just a metaphysical structure but a meditative practice. The yogi traces the twelve phases in every moment of perception, learning to recognize the goddess's play in the most ordinary acts of cognition.

4. **The Centrality of the Goddess**: In the Krama, the goddess is not a subsidiary power but the principal deity. Kālasaṃkarṣiṇī is the supreme consciousness itself, and the Kālīs are her twelve modes of activity.
