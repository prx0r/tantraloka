# CONCERNING FREEDOM AND BONDAGE — Verses 22-35
## 10-Minute Deep-Dive Video Essay

### Core Teaching

Abhinava begins the doctrinal exposition of the Tantrāloka by addressing the fundamental question: what is bondage and what is liberation? He stakes out the nondualist position against the dualist Śaiva Siddhānta. For Siddhānta, bondage is caused by a material substance called mala (Impurity) that can only be removed by ritual action. For Abhinava, following the Pratyabhijñā, bondage is ignorance — a mistaken self-understanding. Liberation is knowledge — the recognition of one's true nature as Śiva. This is the central debate that animates the entire first chapter.

### Key Verses from Tantrāloka

#### Verse 1.22 — The Universal Assertion

**Line ~9705** (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit:**
```
iha tāvat samasteṣu śāstreṣu parigīyate |
ajñānaṁ saṁsṛter hetur jñānaṁ mokṣaikakāraṇam || 22 ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"Well then, it is proclaimed here (unanimously) in all the scriptures that ignorance is the cause of transmigration, and knowledge is the sole cause of liberation (from it)."

**Explanation:**
This verse establishes a universal principle accepted across all schools. Ignorance (ajñāna) causes bondage; knowledge (jñāna) causes liberation. The entire subsequent debate with the Siddhānta hinges on what these terms mean. For Abhinava, ignorance is not merely absence of knowledge but incomplete knowledge — mistaken self-understanding.

#### Verse 1.23 — Impurity as Ignorance

**Line ~9722** (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit:**
```
malam ajñānam icchanti saṁsārāṅkurakāraṇam |
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"(The wise) prefer (the view that) Impurity (mala) is ignorance (not a material substance as the dualists maintain). It is the cause of (Karma, which is) the sprout of transmigratory existence."

**Explanation:**
This verse directly challenges the Siddhānta position. Impurity (mala) is not a material substance that must be physically removed by ritual. It is ignorance — a cognitive error. The source is the Mālinīvijayottara, indicating that even within the Āgamic tradition, there is support for the nondualist view.

#### Verse 1.24-25 — Ignorance Defined

**Line ~9923** (Vol. 1)

**Dyczkowski Translation (from commentary):**
"Ignorance is darkness. It is not a total absence of knowledge; rather it is incomplete knowledge, which consists of (mistakenly) considering what is not the Self (i.e. the body etc.) and what is the Self to be otherwise (than what they really are)."

**Explanation:**
Jayaratha clarifies that ignorance is not a void but a positive misapprehension. It is the feeling of incompleteness (apūrṇamanyatā) — the sense that "I am not enough" — which is the essence of āṇavamala, the fundamental impurity of the individual soul.

#### Verses 1.26-33 — The Hierarchy of Knowledge

**Line ~10600+** (Vol. 1)

**Dyczkowski Translation (from passages):**
"By the second aphorism — that is, by the word 'knowledge' in the second aphorism (which declares that 'knowledge is bondage'). 'Knowledge' is the act of knowing, and the means by which something is known."

**Explanation:**
Abhinava argues that every school attains liberation according to the completeness of its knowledge. Lower schools achieve liberation from lower levels of bondage; only the nondualist teaching of the Trika achieves complete liberation because its knowledge is complete.

#### Verse 1.34-35 — The Nondualist Conclusion

**Line ~11897** (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit (approximate):**
```
yastu sarvātmanā jñānaṁ samagrāvaraṇāpahaḥ |
sa mokṣaḥ paramo jñeyaḥ śivabhūtir anuttarā || 34 ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"But the knowledge that removes all the coverings (āvaraṇa) completely is the supreme liberation; it should be known as the supreme state of Śiva, than which there is none higher."

**Explanation:**
Only complete knowledge — the recognition that one's nature is Śiva — removes all coverings. This is the parama mokṣa, the supreme liberation, which is not a state to be attained but one's own nature to be recognized.

### Commentary from Jayaratha (Viveka)

**On mala as ignorance (line ~9750+):** Jayaratha quotes the Kiraṇa: "Impurity is ignorance and the fettered state. It is variously called 'obscuring darkness', 'ignorance', 'covering', and 'coma'."

**On the three impurities (line ~9968+):** Jayaratha explains: Āṇavamala is the sense of incompleteness; Māyīyamala is perception of duality; Kārmamala is binding action. All three are forms of ignorance, not substances.

**The hierarchy of liberation (line ~11349+):** Jayaratha traces how different schools attain different levels: Buddhists reach intellect-tattva, Sāṁkhyas reach puruṣa-tattva, Yogins reach niyati-tattva, Pāśupatas reach Māyā-tattva. Only nondualist Śaivas attain the supreme state of Śiva.

### Dyczkowski's Notes

**On Siddhānta view (note 346, line ~9752+):** Dyczkowski summarizes Sanderson: the Siddhānta maintains that mala is a material substance that can only be removed by ritual. Knowledge alone cannot liberate because the impurity is physical, like a cataract requiring surgery.

**On the three malas (note 331, line ~9957+):** "Nondualist Śaivas agree that mala exists... However, they maintain that it is essentially nothing but ignorance, and that it can be removed only by knowledge."

### Philosophical Context

This section sets the entire agenda of the Tantrāloka. If bondage is ignorance, and liberation is knowledge, then the primary means to liberation is not ritual (though ritual can help refine the mind) but gnosis — direct recognition of one's nature as Śiva. This is the nondualist position that distinguishes Abhinava's Trika from the dualist Śaiva Siddhānta.

### Visual Concepts

1. **Impurity as Cataract/Ignorance:** Split screen showing physical impurity (dirt, rust) vs. ignorance (a curtain over the eyes), representing the two views.

2. **Hierarchy of Liberation:** An ascending ladder of tattvas with figures at each level, showing the partial liberation of each school, culminating in Śiva tattva.

3. **The Fettered Soul:** A circle that appears complete but is covered by overlapping layers (the three malas), which dissolve one by one as knowledge increases.

### Narration Script

The Tantrāloka begins its doctrinal exposition with the most fundamental question: why are we bound, and how can we be free? Abhinava states the universal principle: ignorance causes bondage; knowledge causes liberation. But the real question is what these terms mean. The dualist Śaiva Siddhānta maintains that ignorance is caused by a material substance — mala, Impurity — which must be physically removed by ritual. Abhinava argues that impurity is ignorance itself. It is not a thing but a mistake — the mistaken sense of being incomplete. Liberation is the recognition of one's true nature as Śiva. This recognition is not a new attainment but the removal of a misunderstanding.
