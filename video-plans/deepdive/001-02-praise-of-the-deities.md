# IN PRAISE OF THE DEITIES — Verses 2-6
## 10-Minute Deep-Dive Video Essay

### Core Teaching

Abhinavagupta opens the exposition of the Tantrāloka proper by invoking the three goddesses of the Trika — Parā, Parāparā, and Aparā — who are the three powers of Bhairava's consciousness. These are not merely deities to be worshipped externally; they are the very dynamics of consciousness itself: will, knowledge, and action. Parā is the supreme power of pure I-consciousness, the creative intuition (citpratibhā) that is Bhairava's own nature. Parāparā is the power of knowledge that mediates between subject and object, burning up the triple fetter of impurity. Aparā is the power of action that resides within Bhairava's dancing form, playing like lightning through the dark storm-cloud of duality.

Together, these three goddesses constitute what Jayaratha calls the "intermediate form" (parāpara rūpa) of the Trika triad. The supreme form (para) is the Heart itself — the union of Śiva and Śakti described in verse 1. The intermediate form is the three goddesses corresponding to subject, means of knowledge, and object. The lower form (apara) is Śiva, Śakti, and Nara — the three phases of emanation. By praising the goddesses, Abhinava is mapping the entire architecture of consciousness in its threefold dynamics of perception.

### Key Verses from Tantrāloka

#### Verse 1.2 — Invocation of Parā

**Line ~3133** (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit:**
```
naumi citpratibhāṁ devīṁ parāṁ bhairavayoginīm |
mātṛmānaprameyāṁśaśūlāmbujakṛtāspadām || 2 ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"I bow to the goddess Parā (the Supreme One), who is the creative intuition of consciousness (citpratibhā), Bhairava's Yoginī (united with Him), She has made (her) place on (top of) the lotuses of the Trika Trident, the prongs of which are the aspects of perception, namely subject, object and means of knowledge."

**Explanation:**
Parā is addressed as citpratibhā — the creative flash of insight that is consciousness itself. She is Bhairava's Yoginī, never separate from Him, the power by which He knows Himself. The Trika Trident is the central symbol of Trika worship, its three prongs representing subject (mātṛ), means (māna), and object (meya). Parā sits on the central lotus above the prongs, indicating that she transcends yet encompasses all three.

#### Verse 1.3 — Invocation of Aparā

**Line ~3370** (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit:**
```
naumi devīṁ śarīrasthāṁ nṛtyato bhairavākṛteḥ |
vidyullekheva saṁsāragahanāmbudharākṛteḥ || 3 ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"I bow to the goddess (Aparā), who resides in the body of Bhairava's dancing form and plays within it (like) a lightning flash in a sky (covered) with dense storm clouds."

**Explanation:**
Aparā is the power of action, the third goddess, who dwells within Bhairava's manifest form. The storm-cloud imagery is deliberate: Bhairava's dark body is the density of manifested duality, and Aparā is the lightning-flash of consciousness that suddenly illumines it. She is the kinetic energy of the universe, the play of consciousness within embodiment.

#### Verse 1.4 — Invocation of Parāparā

**Line ~3783** (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit:**
```
stāj jñānaśūlaṁ satpakṣavipakṣotkartanakṣamam |
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"May (Parāparā), the pulsingly radiant prong of knowledge, that has burnt up the triple fetter with its blazing rays of the light (of Śiva consciousness), have the power to uproot all that runs contrary to the truth."

**Explanation:**
Parāparā is the middle prong of the Trident — the power of knowledge. She burns up the three impurities (Āṇava, Māyīya, Kārma) with the rays of the senses which are themselves forms of her light. She is the mediating power that transforms duality into the realization of nonduality.

#### Verse 1.5 — The Lower Trika

**Line ~3956** (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit:**
```
śivaḥ śaktir naraḥ śaṁbhor vyaktir īdṛgbahirgatā |
yā prayānty anyadākāraṁ devatā mānuṣīm iva || 5 ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"Śiva, Śakti and Nara (the individual soul) manifest as the external (objective) expansion of Śiva (śambhu), just as the deities (when they appear on earth) assume a human form."

**Explanation:**
This verse describes the lower (apara) form of Trika. When the supreme reality externalizes itself, it appears as the triad of Śiva (the transcendent Lord), Śakti (His power), and Nara (the individual soul and the manifest universe). This is not a fall or degradation but a voluntary assumption of form by the formless, "just as deities when they appear on earth assume a human form."

#### Verse 1.6 — The Nature of the Teaching

**Line ~3976** (Vol. 1)

**Sanskrit:**
```
evaṁ mukhyā mukhyakalpā vividhā devatā gaṇāḥ |
tathā ca śāstra iti me bhāvo bhāvāya na svayam || 6 ||
```

**Dyczkowski Translation:**
"In this way, (there are) many groups of deities, principal ones and those like them. And, (my) teaching (śāstra) (is composed in this same variety of ways). This is my intention (bhāva) that leads to the supreme reality (bhāvāya) (in accord with the ways people are), not (the gratification of my) own (self)."

**Explanation:**
Abhinava explains that the diversity of deities and teachings is purposeful — it corresponds to the diversity of human capacities. His intention is not self-display but the spiritual welfare of beings. This anticipates the entire pedagogical structure of the Tantrāloka, which presents multiple means (upāya) for different types of aspirants.

### Commentary from Jayaratha (Viveka)

**On verse 2 (line ~3155+):** Jayaratha explains that Parā is "full (all-encompassing and perfect)" because "even though the universe appears to be separate from Her, She protects it within Herself as one with Herself." He quotes the Mālinīvijayottara: "The power of the Sustainer of the Universe, said to be one with Him, is the Goddess."

**On the Trika Trident (line ~3190+):** Commenting on the trident imagery, Jayaratha explains that the three prongs represent the three aspects of perception (subject, means, object). Supreme nonduality is achieved by including these four aspects — subject, means, object, and knowledge itself — within the one consciousness. "In Abhinava's exegesis of the Trika triad, the first three are the three Goddesses. Parā is the subject; Parāparā is the means of knowledge and Aparā is the object."

**On verse 3 (line ~3419+):** Jayaratha cites the Śivasūtra (3/9) — "the Self is the dancer" — to explain that Bhairava's dancing form is the Supreme Self manifesting the unfolding of each wonderfully diverse role He assumes. Aparā operates on the plane of immanence within the body, senses, mind and the outer world.

**On verse 4 (line ~3792+):** Jayaratha defines knowledge according to the MV: "The power of knowledge is said to make known here in this world what is well determined, namely that: 'this thing is such and not otherwise.'" He explains that the prong of knowledge burns up the triple impurity because "the rays of the senses" are themselves the activity of consciousness.

### Dyczkowski's Notes

**On Parā as citpratibhā (note 59, line ~3161):** "One could also translate 'pratibhā' as 'light', and so the meaning would be that Parā is the 'light of consciousness'."

**On the three forms of Trika (note 53, line ~3143):** "The first verse represents the supreme aspect of the triad of Śiva, Śakti and Nara. At the intermediate level it is the three Goddesses... They also embody Śiva's three powers of will, knowledge and action."

**On verse 5 (note 123, line ~3960):** "The relationship of the Trika triad to perception (subject, means and object) is fundamental to Abhinava's entire system. He will use this hermeneutical tool throughout the Tantrāloka."

### Philosophical Context

The invocation of the three goddesses establishes the Trika framework that governs the entire Tantrāloka. Parā is will (icchā) — the pure intention of consciousness to manifest. Parāparā is knowledge (jñāna) — the power that differentiates and ascertains. Aparā is action (kriyā) — the power that actualizes. These three are not separate but are aspects of the single power of Śiva's freedom (svātantryaśakti), which is the creative intuition (pratibhā) of consciousness itself.

The Trika Trident is the central symbol of this teaching. Its three prongs rise from the navel of Sadāśiva and extend through the crown of the head into the transcendent realm of the Transmental (unmanā). On each prong sits a goddess on a lotus, and on each goddess sits a Bhairava. This visualization is not merely iconographic — it represents the structure of perception itself, which is the structure of reality.

### Visual Concepts

1. **The Trika Trident:** An animated golden trident rising from a lotus at the base, its three prongs branching out. On each prong, a lotus blooms with a goddess seated upon it, colored white (Parā), red (Parāparā), and black (Aparā).

2. **Parā as Lightning of Consciousness:** A dark storm-cloud background (Bhairava's body) with a sudden flash of lightning (Aparā) illuminating the scene, then dissolving into the white light of Parā.

4. **Bhairava Dancing:** A silhouette of a dancing figure against a dark sky, with lightning playing within it, referencing Śivasūtra 3/9.

### Narration Script

**[0:00-1:00 — OPENING]**

After the magnificent invocation of the Heart in verse one, Abhinavagupta begins the actual teaching of the Tantrāloka by praising the three goddesses of the Trika: Parā, Parāparā, and Aparā. These are not simply deities to be worshipped from outside. They are the three powers of consciousness itself — will, knowledge, and action — understood as the very dynamics by which reality unfolds.

The goddess Parā is addressed first. Abhinava calls her citpratibhā — the creative intuition of consciousness. She is Bhairava's own power, never separate from Him. The Trika Trident on which she sits has three prongs, representing the subject who knows, the means by which he knows, and the object he knows. Parā sits above all three, because she is the supreme consciousness that encompasses every act of perception.

**[1:00-3:00 — THE THREE GODDESSES]**

Let us read Abhinava's own words:

"I bow to the goddess Parā, the Supreme One, who is the creative intuition of consciousness, Bhairava's Yoginī. She has made her place on the lotuses of the Trika Trident, the prongs of which are subject, object and means of knowledge."

Jayaratha explains that Parā is "full" — all-encompassing and perfect. Even though the universe appears to be separate from her, she protects it within herself as one with herself. She is the power of the sustainer of the universe, one with Bhairava himself.

The second goddess is Aparā, the lower one — but "lower" only in the sense that she operates on the plane of embodiment and duality. Abhinava describes her with extraordinary poetic force:

"I bow to the goddess who resides in the body of Bhairava's dancing form, playing within it like a lightning flash in a sky covered with dense storm clouds."

The dark storm-cloud is the density of manifested reality — the entire world of names and forms. Aparā is the lightning of consciousness that flashes through it, illuminating it from within. Jayaratha quotes the Śivasūtra: "The Self is the dancer." Bhairava dances the cosmic dance, and Aparā is the energy of his movement.

The third goddess is Parāparā — the "Supreme-and-not-Supreme," the power of knowledge that mediates between pure subjectivity and pure objectivity. She is the middle prong of the Trident, and Abhinava prays:

"May Parāparā, the pulsingly radiant prong of knowledge, that has burnt up the triple fetter with its blazing rays, have the power to uproot all that runs contrary to the truth."

The triple fetter is the three impurities — Āṇava, the sense of individual incompleteness; Māyīya, the perception of duality; and Kārma, the binding force of past actions. Parāparā burns them with the rays of the senses, which are themselves forms of her light.

**[3:00-5:00 — THE LOWER TRIKA]**

Abhinava then describes the lower form of Trika in verse five. Here the triad is Śiva, Śakti, and Nara — the individual soul. He says that these three manifest as the external expansion of Śiva, just as deities when they appear on earth assume a human form. The point is profound: the entire universe of subjects and objects is the voluntary self-limitation of the absolute.

Abhinava concludes this section with a remarkable statement of intent. He says in verse six that his teaching is presented in this variety of ways corresponding to the variety of human dispositions, and that his intention is "not self-display but the leading to the supreme reality." In a single verse, he declares the pedagogical heart of the entire Tantrāloka: multiple paths for multiple people, all leading to the same goal.

**[5:00-8:00 — THE TRIKA TRIDENT IN DEPTH]**

The Trident is the central symbol of the Trika. In ritual practice, it is visualized rising from the navel of Sadāśiva, passing through the heart and throat, and emerging from the crown of the head. Its three prongs extend up to the Transmental state beyond ordinary consciousness. On each prong sits a goddess, and on each goddess sits a Bhairava.

The three goddesses correspond to the three powers of consciousness: will, knowledge, and action. But each power contains the other two within itself. Parā, though primarily will, is also knowledge and action in their undifferentiated state. Parāparā, though primarily knowledge, is threaded through with will and action. Aparā, though primarily action, is inseparable from will and knowledge.

Abhinava uses these three goddesses to establish a hermeneutical tool that he will apply throughout the entire Tantrāloka: the analysis of every reality — from the highest tattvas down to the gross elements — in terms of subject, means, and object. This is the Trika method: to see the threefold structure of consciousness in everything.

**[8:00-9:30 — THE PHILOSOPHICAL FRAMEWORK]**

The three goddesses are not ultimately separate from one another. Parā, as the supreme, encompasses both Parāparā and Aparā within herself. The three are one power — the svātantryaśakti, the power of absolute freedom — appearing in three aspects.

This is why, in the Trika maṇḍala, Parā is always in the center. The three prongs of the Trident merge into a single point at the base, which is the Heart — the union of Śiva and Śakti. The diversity of the three goddesses is the play of the one consciousness within itself.

As Dyczkowski notes, this framework of subject-means-object is not merely a philosophical abstraction. It is the structure of every moment of perception. When you see a blue flower, there is the subject (the perceiver), the means (the eyes and mind), and the object (the flower). But underlying all three is the light of consciousness itself — Parā — which makes the entire event possible.

**[9:30-10:00 — CLOSING]**

Abhinava begins his great work by invoking this triadic structure because it is the key to everything that follows. Whether he is discussing the thirty-six tattvas, the three upāyas, or the most esoteric Kaula rites, the triadic framework of subject-means-object will be his constant analytical tool.

The three goddesses are thus not merely an opening invocation — they are the gateway to the entire system. Parā, Parāparā, and Aparā are the Prong of Will, the Prong of Knowledge, and the Prong of Action. Together they are the Trika Trident, and the Trident is the very structure of reality as consciousness.

### Connections

- **Powers of Śiva's Freedom (1.66cd-80):** The three goddesses are explicitly identified with Śiva's svātantryaśakti — the single power that appears as will, knowledge, and action.
- **The Twelve Kālīs (1.106cd-115ab):** Each of the three goddesses operates in four phases (creation, persistence, withdrawal, and the Fourth), producing the twelve Kālīs of the Krama system.
- **Triadic Perception:** The subject-means-object hermeneutic will govern the analysis of the tattvas, upāyas, and ritual practice throughout the Tantrāloka.
