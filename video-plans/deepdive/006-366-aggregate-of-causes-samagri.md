# 366 — The Sāmagrī Argument: Aggregate of Causal Factors
**Tantrāloka 9.30–35ab** | Chapter 9: Kāryakāraṇa (Causality)

## Hook
A pot requires clay, water, wheel, and potter. But these factors do not assemble themselves. What brings them together?

## Core Teaching
The *sāmagrī* (aggregate of causal factors) requires a unifying agent. No effect arises from a single cause; multiple factors must converge. This convergence cannot happen by itself — insentient factors cannot coordinate their own activity. Only consciousness can unify the causal field.

## The Problem of Multiple Causes
Every effect has multiple causes:
1. Material cause (*upādāna kāraṇa*) — the substance from which it is made
2. Efficient cause (*nimitta kāraṇa*) — the agent who makes it
3. Instrumental causes (*karaṇa*) — the tools used
4. Formal cause — the idea or plan in the agent's mind

These factors are diverse, separate, and have no intrinsic relation to each other.

## The Trika Solution
The real agent who unifies the *sāmagrī* is Supreme Śiva:
- He is present in the clay as its material nature
- He is present in the potter as his consciousness
- He is present in the tools as their functional capacity
- He is present in the intention as the will to create

## The Potter as Apparent Agent
The potter's agency is apparent, not real:
- He depends on clay (cannot make a pot from water)
- He depends on tools (cannot shape clay with his hands alone)
- He depends on his body (which he did not create)
- His "I made this" is the power of Śiva functioning through him

## Undivided Agency
All causal factors share in the one agency of Śiva. The *sāmagrī* is not a collection but a distribution of the one power across multiple loci. The effect arises from the single intention of Śiva manifesting as the coordinated activity of all factors.

## Connection to Non-Successive Consciousness
The *sāmagrī* argument demonstrates that causality is not a mechanical sequence but a conscious act. All factors of the cause exist simultaneously in the consciousness of Śiva before they manifest sequentially in time. This anticipates the Pratyabhijñā doctrine of recognition: the effect was never separate from the cause.

## Connection to Chapter 10
Just as the *sāmagrī* requires a unifying agent, the perceptibility of an object to multiple perceivers requires a single consciousness that manifests as all perceivers. The same logic—unity within diversity—governs both causality and perception.
