# Deep-Dive Video Essay: Collective Consciousness in the Ādiyāga Assembly

**Source:** Tantrāloka Vol. 10, Chapter 28, Verses 18cd-25ab (~lines 1840-2065)
**Duration:** 10 minutes

## Opening Hook (0:00-0:45)
Abhinavagupta presents one of his most original contributions in these verses: a phenomenological analysis of collective consciousness during ritual assembly. Using the analogy of a theatre audience, he explains how even an uninvited latecomer can instantly share in the collective realization of Siddhas and Yoginīs. This is consciousness-as-field theory avant la lettre.

## Part 1: The Assembly of the Illumined (0:45-3:00)
- Verses 18-19 (~line 1851): "taccakracāraniṣṇātā ye kecit pūrṇasaṁvidaḥ | tanmelakasamāyuktās te tatpūjāparāḥ sadā"
- Those well-versed in the movement of the wheels (cakracāra) of breath, senses, and time have full consciousness
- They are always intent on worship in the assembly (melaka) of Siddhas and Yoginīs
- "Cakracāra" has two registers:
  1. The cycles of breath homologized to time (from Chapter 6)
  2. The activity of the wheels of sensory deities
- Being "well-versed" means experiencing through the senses that everything is the outpouring of one's own consciousness — this nourishes and "fills" it

## Part 2: The Transformative Power of the Assembly (3:00-5:30)
- Verse 19cd-20ab: Even one whose consciousness is *atanmaya* (not co-extensive with the collective consciousness) becomes *tanmaya* (one with it) at that time
- This happens through two means:
  1. Worship according to one's own Kula liturgy (svakramārcana)
  2. Simply by joining (melaka) the assembly of Siddhas and Yoginīs
- The melaka is both a gathering for collective worship and — potentially — a union (including physical union) of male Siddhas and female Yoginīs
- Jayaratha notes: the word melaka can mean both "meeting" and "union"

## Part 3: The Theatre Analogy (5:30-7:30)
- Verses 20-22 (~line 1997): "yathā prekṣaṇake tattaddraṣṭrsaṁvidabheditām | kramoditāṁ sadya eva labhate tatpraveśanāt"
- **The analogy:** In a theatre, each spectator's consciousness gradually becomes unified through watching the show. When a latecomer enters, they *immediately* share in this unified consciousness.
- **The application:** Similarly, by entering the assembly, one immediately obtains full, unconditioned consciousness that the members have attained gradually through yoga practice.
- "And so is it also with that time (on the sacred day) which, because consciousness is then full, is like the cow that grants all desires" (verse 22)
- By worshipping well at that time, one quickly attains every fruit one may desire

## Part 4: The Uninvited Guest — Grace Beyond Merit (7:30-9:30)
- Verses 23-25 (~line 2048): "yathā ciropāttadhanaḥ kurvann utsavam ādarāṭ | atithiṁ ṣo 'nugṛhṇāti tatkālābhijñam āgatam"
- **Second analogy:** One who has saved money for a long time throws a party. When a guest arrives who knows the time of the party (even if uninvited), the host graciously welcomes him.
- Similarly, the leaders of the Yoginīs and Siddhas, although they exert themselves for their own fruits, grace anyone who comes knowing that it is the time of the assembly
- This is a theology of grace: the plenitude of the assembly overflows to include even the unprepared
- Jayaratha calls this Abhinava's own reasoning, then cites the Haiḍara as scriptural support

## Conclusion (9:30-10:00)
This passage is unique in Tantric literature for its phenomenological analysis of group consciousness. The assembly becomes a field of collective realization where individual boundaries dissolve.
