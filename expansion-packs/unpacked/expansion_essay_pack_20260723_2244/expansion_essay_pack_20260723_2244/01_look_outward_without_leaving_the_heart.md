# look outward without leaving the heart

Open your eyes.

The room arrives immediately.

Edges, colors, distance, movement. Attention runs outward and fastens itself to things before you have decided what matters.

Now close your eyes.

The room withdraws. Sensation becomes interior. Memory, pressure, sound, darkness. Attention seems to return to the one who was looking.

Kashmir Śaivism refuses the choice.

Its highest state is not eyes open or eyes closed.

It is the condition in which the world remains visible without awareness abandoning its source.

Look outward.

Do not leave the heart.

---

Ordinary attention is built around alternation.

When the outer world becomes vivid, inward awareness fades. When meditation deepens, objects recede. Engagement and absorption appear to compete for the same narrow doorway.

Abhinavagupta inherits several technical names for these movements.

*Nimīlana* is closing: the senses withdraw, distinctions soften, and awareness contracts toward its undivided ground.

*Unmīlana* is opening: consciousness expands into perception, action, and the differentiated world.

Neither is rejected.

The problem begins when they are treated as mutually exclusive states.

If truth requires withdrawal, ordinary life becomes exile.

If the world alone is real, the source of perception disappears behind its objects.

Tantric practice attempts to preserve both directions at once.

---

The integrated condition is described through terms such as *pratimīlana-samādhi*, *bhairavīmudrā*, and, in related analyses, *kramamudrā*.

The vocabulary varies across texts and commentaries, but the central dynamic is clear.

Awareness sinks into its own depth.

Then it opens into the world.

The opening does not erase the depth.

The depth does not cancel the world.

The movement becomes a circuit.

Absorption externalizes itself as perception.

Perception returns into the consciousness that made it possible.

The yogin learns to inhabit the turning rather than choosing one side.

---

This sounds simple until you watch attention closely.

Look at an object.

A cup.

A tree.

A face.

Ordinarily, awareness seems to travel across a gap and land upon something outside itself. The object gains clarity. The one looking becomes almost invisible.

Now redirect attention toward the fact of seeing.

The object may blur or become secondary. Awareness feels located inwardly, behind or within the gaze.

You have moved from seen to seer.

But the tradition asks for a third possibility.

Can the object remain completely visible while the luminous fact of seeing remains equally present?

Not half the attention on each.

Not rapid switching.

One event containing both poles.

---

Kṣemarāja preserves an image of this state through a verse describing the powers of seeing and the other senses moving toward their objects while the practitioner remains established within like an unmoving golden pillar.

The pillar does not prevent perception.

It gives perception a center.

The senses can extend everywhere without dragging identity behind them.

Vision reaches the form.

Hearing reaches the sound.

Touch reaches the surface.

Yet the one field from which these powers emerge is not forgotten.

The world expands around a still axis.

---

This is why *mudrā* here is more than a hand gesture.

A mudrā is a seal, a configuration, a way consciousness holds itself.

Bhairavīmudrā seals the inward and outward into one stance.

The eyes may be open. The body may move. Conversation may continue. Nothing needs to appear mystical from outside.

The difference lies in the relation between center and extension.

Ordinary perception loses itself in what it perceives.

Withdrawn meditation protects the center by reducing perception.

The Tantric seal allows the center to become the power of perception itself.

---

*Kramamudrā* emphasizes sequence and circulation.

The Sanskrit *krama* means succession, step, ordered progression.

Consciousness enters absorption, emerges into manifestation, recognizes itself in the manifested form, and returns without rupture.

The process can repeat until the boundary between meditation and activity becomes less convincing.

This does not mean every distracted state is already realization.

The sequence must become transparent.

The practitioner recognizes the same awareness in contraction and expansion, silence and sound, inwardness and contact.

The pulse changes direction.

Its substance does not change.

---

Imagine breathing through a piece of cloth.

Air passes outward.

Air passes inward.

The cloth does not travel with either current. It remains the place through which both movements occur.

The heart of awareness functions similarly, though it is not a physical object in the chest.

Perceptions emerge through it.

Thoughts return through it.

Attention expands and contracts.

The center is not one phase of the movement.

It is the capacity for movement in both directions.

When awareness identifies only with the outward current, the world feels alien and captivating.

When it identifies only with withdrawal, the world feels like interruption.

Recognition discovers the aperture.

---

This makes the practice radically different from dissociation.

Dissociation often reduces contact with body, emotion, or environment. The person feels unreal, distant, or absent.

Bhairavīmudrā intensifies contact while loosening exclusive identification.

The color is more present, not less.

The body remains felt.

The other person is not converted into a dream figure.

What changes is the assumption that contact requires losing the ground of awareness.

Presence becomes wider than fixation.

The room is vivid.

The center is not gone.

---

Nor is the practice a permanent wide-angle stare.

Forcing visual attention can create strain. The point is not to hold the eyes unnaturally still or attempt an exotic neurological state.

The practice concerns recognition within ordinary perception.

Begin with hearing.

Let every sound arrive without selecting one as the enemy of silence.

Traffic.

A fan.

A voice.

A bird.

Notice that the sounds differ, but their appearing does not need to compete with awareness of the field in which they appear.

Silence is not located behind the sounds.

It is the openness that allows sound to be distinct.

The senses move outward.

The field does not move away.

---

Abhinavagupta’s nondualism makes this possible because subject and object are not absolutely separate substances.

The object is not invented by the personal mind.

Yet its appearance occurs within consciousness, through the powers of consciousness, as a determinate disclosure.

The seer, seeing, and seen are differentiated positions within one manifesting event.

Ordinary cognition notices the positions and misses the event.

Bhairavīmudrā notices the event without erasing the positions.

The tree remains tree.

You remain a finite observer.

But the relation between you is no longer imagined as a bridge thrown across absolute darkness.

Both shine within one field.

---

This is the contemplative meaning of bi-directional gaze.

The gaze moves outward as perception.

It moves inward as recognition.

At maturity, these are no longer two separate operations.

Seeing the object becomes awareness seeing its own power to appear as object, seer, and relation.

The world is not pulled back into a private interior.

The interior is discovered to be larger than privacy.

The heart is not inside the body like an organ in a container.

The body and world appear within the heart’s power of manifestation.

---

The ethical consequence matters.

When awareness loses itself in objects, the object becomes something to seize, avoid, classify, or use. Perception is immediately recruited by appetite.

When awareness withdraws from the world, the other person can become spiritually irrelevant.

Integrated perception allows intimacy without capture.

The object is fully encountered but not reduced to a supply for the self.

The other is allowed to appear inside consciousness without being treated as a possession of consciousness.

Nonduality becomes a discipline of relation.

Not “you are only me.”

Rather:

The difference between us appears within a field deeper than ownership.

---

This helps explain why Abhinavagupta’s liberated state is aesthetic.

The world can be enjoyed because enjoyment no longer requires forgetting the source of experience.

Color, taste, rhythm, touch, and thought become expressions rather than traps.

The senses do not need to be starved into obedience.

They need to be divinized—recognized as powers of awareness rather than instruments of compulsive acquisition.

Bhairavīmudrā is the stance that allows the feast without losing the host.

---

Try the simplest version.

Look at the room.

Let attention include one clear object.

Then include the edges of vision.

Then include bodily sensation.

Then include the fact that all of this is known.

Do not search for a separate knower.

Let knowing remain implicit and undeniable.

Object.

Field.

Body.

Awareness.

One event, internally differentiated.

If strain appears, release the technique. The natural state is not produced by force.

The exercise is only a way of noticing what effort usually divides.

---

Open your eyes.

The room arrives.

Ordinarily, you leave with it. Attention runs into shapes and forgets where visibility began.

Close your eyes.

The room withdraws. Ordinarily, you call the absence inwardness.

But the heart was present in both movements.

It opened as the room.

It closed as interiority.

It remained through the turn.

Look outward without leaving the heart.

Not because the world is inside your personal mind.

Because the power by which “inside” and “outside” become distinct was never confined to either side.

The eyes remain open.

The center has stopped disappearing.
