# matter remembers by becoming easier to change again

Bend a paperclip.

Straighten it.

The metal looks almost restored.

Bend it again, and the old deformation returns more easily.

The past is no longer visible as a picture.

It survives as altered readiness.

This is the deepest form of memory.

Not a recording of what happened.

A change in what can happen next.

Matter remembers by becoming easier to change again.

---

Memory is usually imagined through storage.

An event occurs.

A representation is written.

The representation waits.

Retrieval opens the archive and plays the past again.

This model works well for files and photographs.

It works less well for many physical and biological systems.

A crumpled sheet remembers previous folds through stress patterns.

A magnet remembers the history of applied fields.

A glass remembers how it was cooled.

A suspension trained through repeated shear can encode the amplitude of that shear and later reveal it through its response.

No miniature image of the training event is stored.

The system has been reshaped into a different responder.

---

Nathan Keim, Sidney Nagel, and collaborators surveyed these phenomena under the title “Memory formation in matter.”

Their key point is that memory often belongs to systems away from equilibrium.

At equilibrium, much historical information is erased.

The system settles into a state determined by present constraints rather than the detailed path taken to reach them.

Out of equilibrium, history can remain encoded in metastable arrangements, hysteresis, particle contacts, defect structures, and response thresholds.

The present state carries readable consequences of earlier driving.

Memory is persistence of history in dynamics.

---

Mechanical memory makes this visible.

Take an amorphous solid and repeatedly deform it to one amplitude.

Local particles rearrange.

With training, the system can settle into a reversible cycle below that amplitude.

Later, increasing the deformation reveals a sharp change near the trained value.

The material has learned a threshold.

It does not remember through symbols.

Its configuration has been sculpted so that one future perturbation produces a distinctive response.

Training writes a question into matter.

The readout asks the same question again.

---

A 2024 review by Paulsen and Keim describes how such memories arise in disordered solids and can be designed into mechanical metamaterials.

The distinction between memory and damage is subtle.

Both are history-dependent change.

Memory becomes useful when the change is readable, stable enough, and capable of guiding a later response.

A dent is a trace.

A trained material can be a computation.

Its structure transforms future input according to past experience.

The material does not need a brain to be history-sensitive.

It needs degrees of freedom that can be reorganized and later interrogated.

---

Dense active matter extends the idea.

A 2024 arXiv study examined particles driven by locally correlated motion. The system could encode memories of repeated forcing, and cooperative motion changed how efficiently memories formed.

The driving was not one uniform external shear.

It was spatially patterned, closer to the internally generated activity of living collectives.

Memory capacity depended upon how movements were correlated across space.

This matters biologically.

The past may not be stored in one component.

It can be distributed across relations among many active parts.

The trace is a collective susceptibility.

---

Living memory intensifies this structure.

A past event alters gene expression, synaptic strength, receptor density, tissue mechanics, immune readiness, hormonal regulation, attention, and action policy.

Years later, the original event is absent.

The organism encounters the changed mapping it left behind.

A sound produces tension faster.

A scent evokes orientation.

A practiced hand reaches correctly without replaying every training session.

The past survives as a bias in the transformation from input to response.

Memory is a policy written into flesh.

---

This gives *saṃskāra* unusual precision.

The Sanskrit term can mean formation, impression, conditioning, or a residual tendency produced by previous action. Its Pali relative *saṅkhāra* has a broader and context-dependent range, including formations, preparations, and volitional activities.

Neither should be translated simply as a memory file.

A saṃskāra is closer to modified readiness.

Previous processes leave the system disposed to construct future experience in a particular way.

The past is present as a constraint.

Not:

This happened.

More primitively:

When something like this happens, attend here, feel this, and prepare that response.

---

This explains why habits and memories blur.

Learning a musical phrase changes what the fingers can do.

Trauma changes what the body predicts.

Meditation changes how quickly attention is captured.

The remembered content may be vague or absent.

The readiness remains exact.

A person can forget the original scene while living inside the policy it produced.

The archive is missing.

The search algorithm has been altered.

What returns is not the past.

It is the present behaving according to a past-written rule.

---

Michael Levin and colleagues describe living systems as self-referential and self-modifying.

A 2025 arXiv paper on time and self-reference distinguishes natural time—the unfolding of physical processes—from representational time, through which living systems organize past, present, and future.

Memory and prediction do not merely add content to life.

They allow the present system to act according to states that are absent.

The past constrains the present.

The anticipated future constrains the present.

The organism becomes a loop stretched across time.

Its current action is partly addressed to versions of itself that do not exist now.

---

This is why memory is self-improvising.

The future self is not identical to the past self.

The body changes.

Goals change.

Context changes.

The trace must be interpreted by a system different from the one that formed it.

A childhood fear returns in an adult body.

The original danger may be gone.

The trace still recruits protection.

Remembering is therefore not copying.

It is reconstruction under present constraints.

The current organism asks:

What does this old alteration mean for the future I can build now?

The same trace can become warning, symptom, story, skill, or irrelevant noise.

---

Material memory clarifies this without reducing human experience to bent metal.

A trained solid does not interpret its past through narrative.

A person does.

But both reveal that history can persist as changed response rather than stored image.

The difference lies in the depth of reconstruction.

Human memory recruits language, social identity, emotion, imagination, and counterfactual reasoning.

The trace enters a system capable of assigning it new significance.

Biological memory is not only persistence.

It is persistence made available to action.

Human remembering adds persistence made available to self-understanding.

---

Ñāṇavīra’s analysis of *saṅkhāra* and temporality sharpens the point.

A thing persists not because every component remains fixed, but because a higher-order pattern remains invariant across subordinate transformations.

Memory participates in this persistence.

The organism now differs from the organism then.

A trace allows the later system to respond as the transformed continuation of the earlier one.

Identity is not a substance carrying records through time.

It is a structured continuity in which prior transformations constrain later ones.

The past belongs to the present because the present is one lawful transformation of the system the past altered.

---

The Pratyabhijñā philosophers add an objection.

A trace can explain why a present image or reaction occurs.

Can it explain recognition?

“I experienced this before.”

The statement does more than reproduce information.

It connects an earlier experience and a present act as belonging to one subject.

Utpaladeva argues that traces alone cannot generate this first-person unity.

The trace is present now.

The earlier cognition is gone.

Recognition requires a subject spanning the difference.

Buddhist process accounts answer through causal continuity.

Śaiva accounts posit reflexive consciousness as the unity through which continuity is owned.

The dispute begins exactly where physical memory stops.

---

Matter can remember without saying “mine.”

A magnet can retain hysteresis.

A folded sheet can preserve a crease.

A cell can change its future response.

The first-person form of memory introduces another layer.

The trace is not only reactivated.

It is encountered as part of my history.

This may depend upon self-models, narrative reconstruction, and neural integration.

Abhinavagupta claims those mechanisms appear within a deeper *vimarśa*: awareness’s power to relate manifestations to itself.

Science can map the trace.

Recognition philosophy asks what makes the trace present as mine.

---

The practical consequence is merciful.

You do not need to delete the past to change its power.

A memory-policy can be retrained.

The old cue can occur in a safer context.

The body can remain present without completing the old response.

New actions can alter which future the trace predicts.

The goal is not perfect forgetting.

It is changing the readiness the past created.

A scar may remain.

The tissue around it can learn another movement.

---

Bend a paperclip.

Straighten it.

The old deformation remains as altered probability.

Your own history also survives in thresholds, tensions, attractions, and reflexes.

Memory is not the past hidden inside you.

It is the present made asymmetrical by what has happened.

Matter remembers by becoming easier to change again.

Freedom begins when the system can be changed by something other than the event that first trained it.

The trace remains true as history.

It stops being sovereign as instruction.
