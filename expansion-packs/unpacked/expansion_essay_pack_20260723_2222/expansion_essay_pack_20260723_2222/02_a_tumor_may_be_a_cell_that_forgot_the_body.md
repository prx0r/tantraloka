# a tumor may be a cell that forgot the body

A cancer cell is not dead matter.

It is alive enough to divide.

Alive enough to move.

Alive enough to alter its surroundings, recruit blood vessels, evade control, and preserve its own lineage.

The problem is not absence of biological competence.

The competence has narrowed.

A cell that once participated in a body begins acting as though its local future were the only future that matters.

One frontier view of cancer asks whether malignancy can be understood partly as a breakdown in multicellular communication.

The cell has not forgotten how to live.

It may have forgotten the scale at which it belongs.

---

Mainstream cancer biology studies mutations, gene regulation, metabolism, immune interaction, tissue microenvironment, and evolutionary selection among cell populations. These approaches are essential and supported by large bodies of evidence.

Developmental bioelectricity adds another layer.

Non-neural cells maintain electrical voltages across their membranes through ion channels and pumps. Cells connected by gap junctions can form tissue-scale electrical networks.

These voltage patterns influence proliferation, migration, differentiation, gene expression, and anatomical development.

The membrane is not only a fence.

It is an electrical decision surface.

---

Researchers in Michael Levin’s lab and related fields have studied how resting membrane potential, *V*mem, changes during development, regeneration, and tumor-like growth.

In frog models, cells expressing oncogenes can develop depolarized electrical states before visible tumor-like structures appear. Experimental manipulation of ion channels has, in some studies, reduced tumor formation even when the oncogenic signal remained present.

Other experiments have induced metastasis-like behavior in genetically ordinary pigment cells through altered bioelectric signaling, including effects initiated in distant cells.

These results are striking.

They are also limited.

Much of the foundational in-vivo evidence comes from amphibian models, not human clinical treatment.

No one should interpret this research as an established method for electrically curing cancer in people.

---

The importance lies in the causal question.

If an oncogene is present but a change in bioelectric state alters whether a tumor-like structure develops, then genetic information does not act alone.

Context matters.

Cellular voltage can change downstream transcription, signaling molecules, and interactions across tissue.

The same genome can participate in different behaviors depending upon the physiological network in which it is embedded.

Mutation may load the weapon.

The tissue state helps determine whether and how it fires.

---

This resembles older observations in cancer biology.

Malignant cells can sometimes behave differently when placed into embryonic or regenerative environments. Tissue architecture, extracellular matrix, immune signals, neighboring cells, and developmental cues can suppress or redirect behavior.

Cancer is therefore not always best imagined as one evil cell containing a complete independent program.

It can also be studied as a failure of organization.

The cell receives, ignores, or misinterprets information about the larger body.

The neighborhood changes what the resident believes is possible.

---

Calling this “forgetting” is a metaphor.

Cells do not necessarily remember the organism through conscious images.

They participate in maintained states.

Electrical coupling, chemical gradients, mechanical forces, and gene networks help establish which behaviors are normal for a cell in a particular location.

A tissue carries expectations in its structure.

Divide here.

Stop here.

Migrate there.

Become this cell type.

Repair this boundary.

When these constraints weaken, local cellular goals can escape.

The cell is not morally rebellious.

Its regulatory world has changed.

---

The traffic-jam analogy captures part of the problem.

A traffic jam cannot be explained only by opening engines and inspecting pistons. Every car may function normally at the component level while the road network fails collectively.

Cancer is not merely a traffic jam, and mutations matter in ways cars do not capture. But the image reveals why organization deserves study.

A multicellular body is a society of cells.

Its health depends not only upon the internal machinery of each citizen but upon signals coordinating behavior across the whole.

The disease may exist partly in relations.

---

Bioelectric networks are especially interesting because they can store distributed state.

The voltage of one cell depends upon ion-channel activity, but connected cells influence one another. Patterns can become stable across groups.

This allows a tissue to behave less like isolated switches and more like a network with collective attractors—preferred configurations toward which activity returns.

A healthy anatomical pattern may correspond to one attractor.

A tumor-supporting state may correspond to another.

Therapy, in this speculative systems view, would not only destroy abnormal cells.

It might shift the network into a different basin.

---

This is the idea of normalization.

Instead of treating every malignant cell as irreversibly committed, researchers ask whether some pathological behaviors can be reprogrammed by restoring instructive context.

The concept is not unique to bioelectricity. Differentiation therapy, immune modulation, and microenvironmental approaches also attempt to change state rather than only kill cells.

Bioelectric work contributes tools and hypotheses concerning membrane voltage, ion channels, gap junctions, serotonin transport, butyrate signaling, and long-range physiological coordination.

It treats form as an active regulatory achievement.

---

The danger is overstatement.

“Cancer is electrical” is no more sufficient than “cancer is genetic.”

Cancer is not one disease with one mechanism. Human tumors vary enormously by tissue, mutation, immune environment, metabolism, developmental origin, and clinical history.

Ion channels are involved in many cancers, but their roles differ. A voltage change may be cause, consequence, marker, or part of a feedback loop.

Animal-model normalization does not guarantee safe human therapy.

The responsible claim is narrower:

Bioelectric state is a biologically important control layer that can interact with oncogenesis and metastasis and may offer diagnostic or therapeutic opportunities.

---

The philosophical force survives the caution.

A cell’s identity is not located entirely in its genome.

A neuron and a liver cell can share DNA while living radically different lives because regulation, history, and context select different possibilities.

Cancer intensifies this principle.

The question “What is this cell?” cannot be answered only by listing its genes.

We must also ask:

What signals does it receive?

Which collective boundaries does it respect?

What future is its behavior organized to preserve?

Identity is relational.

---

This makes the boundary of the self biologically consequential.

In a healthy organism, cells sacrifice local possibilities for the larger whole. They stop dividing. They differentiate. They undergo programmed death. They perform specialized work that may not maximize their own lineage.

The body exists because cellular freedom is constrained into cooperation.

Cancer can involve the collapse of this larger identity.

The local lineage becomes the operative self.

Its success becomes the organism’s failure.

A smaller goal captures the machinery of life.

---

This language must not become moral psychology.

People with cancer did not become ill because they were selfish, insufficiently integrated, or spiritually disconnected.

Tumors are not metaphors sent by the universe.

The biological comparison should never be used to assign blame.

Its value lies in systems reasoning.

Competent parts can produce destructive outcomes when coordination across scales breaks down.

The same structural fact appears in ecosystems, institutions, and economies without making the diseases identical.

---

It also changes the image of control.

A body is not governed by one throne.

No central cell commands every other cell.

Order is maintained through distributed communication and layered constraints.

The organism is a collective achievement renewed continuously.

Health is not static obedience.

It is successful negotiation among billions of agents with local information.

The body survives because its parts repeatedly agree, through physiology rather than language, about the scale of the problem.

---

What would it mean to remind a cell of the body?

In experimental biology, the answer must be molecular and testable.

Alter membrane potential.

Restore gap-junction communication.

Change extracellular signals.

Reconstruct tissue architecture.

Recruit immune recognition.

Shift gene expression.

The phrase “remember the body” is useful only if it leads back to mechanisms.

Otherwise it becomes poetry pretending to be treatment.

---

Yet good scientific metaphors can open new experiments.

If cancer is partly a problem of geometry and information, researchers can ask questions overlooked by a purely cell-autonomous model.

Can electrical signatures reveal pre-tumor states?

Can distant tissues influence metastatic behavior?

Can oncogene-bearing cells be pushed toward normal participation?

Which physiological network states stabilize healthy anatomy?

These are empirical questions.

Their answers may support, modify, or reject the larger theory.

---

A tumor may be a cell that forgot the body.

Not because the cell once contemplated the whole and lost a memory.

Because the mechanisms connecting local action to organism-level goals have been altered.

The cell remains powerful.

The horizon of power has shrunk.

It divides, moves, and adapts inside a world that no longer includes enough of the organism’s future.

The deepest promise of bioelectric cancer research is not an easy cure.

It is a different object of study.

Not only the damaged component.

The broken conversation.

Not only how to kill the cell.

How the body once convinced it to belong.
